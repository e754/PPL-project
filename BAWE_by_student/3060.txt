This essay will start with a history and definition of operant conditioning . It will outline how this technique can be used as an animal care and management tool for non-human primates within captive environments including : veterinary care , husbandry procedures , maternal care , and behaviour modification . Specific applications will be outlined , within both laboratory and zoological settings . The essay will finish with an overview of the value of operant conditioning in terms of a quantitative assessment of its effectiveness and a discussion of any potential costs of training . Operant conditioning has begun to be recognised as a valuable animal management tool to gain the voluntary cooperation of the animals in question -LRB- Laule et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Underlying operant conditioning is the principle that behaviour is determined by consequences -LRB- Laule et al. , 2003 -RRB- . This involves two basic alternatives - positive and negative reinforcement . In simple terms , positive reinforcement involves rewarding an animal for an appropriate response to a cue -LRB- Pryor , 2002 ; Mellen and Ellis , 1996 -RRB- . Negative reinforcement involves an animal giving a correct response to avoid something unpleasant -LRB- Mellen and Ellis , 1996 ; Pryor , 2002 -RRB- . The essay will focus on positive reinforcement training -LRB- PRT -RRB- as an option that should be exhausted before negative reinforcement is considered -LRB- Laule , et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Operant conditioning is far more widely applied in North American as opposed to British Zoos -LRB- Prescott , pers . comm . -RRB- . PRT is still not used as widely as it could be -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . This may be due to lack of information on how to train -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- and a lack of quantitative assessment regarding time investment , costs and benefits of training -LRB- McKinley , et al. , 2003 ; Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 ; Schapiro et al. , 2003 -RRB- . These issues will be returned to later in the essay . PRT has provided alternatives to invasive veterinary procedures . Reinhardt -LRB- 2003 -RRB- documents its use for the acquisition of blood samples from rhesus macaques -LRB- Macaca mulatta -RRB- within a laboratory setting . Traditional methods involved enforced restraint , which raised normative levels of a range of hormones , enzymes and blood readings -LRB- including cortisol , Reinhardt , 2003 -RRB- . The macaques were therefore trained to cooperate during blood collection and voluntarily offer their leg for the injection , reducing cortisol levels significantly -LRB- Reinhardt , 2003 -RRB- . PRT has also been used to acquire urine samples . This technique may offer an alternative to the blood sampling outlined above , in which the crush cage was still used for the early stages of training -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . McKinley et al. -LRB- 2003 -RRB- refined , through training , the species-specific behaviour of scent marking in common marmosets -LRB- Callithrix jaccus -RRB- in a laboratory to obtain urine samples on demand . Once they were trained to scent-mark on designated areas within the cage , holes were drilled at these sites to allow for the insertion of collection vials . PRT has also been incorporated into a range of veterinary procedures within zoological settings . Colahan and Breder -LRB- 2003 -RRB- document the use of PRT with primates at Disney 's Animal Kingdom -LRB- DAS -RRB- . Where immobilisation is necessary , primates have been trained to receive injections or to take oral anaesthetics -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . However , training to present body-parts and desensitisation of medical treatments and instruments has negated the need for immobilisation in many cases . For example , a mandrill was trained to position an injured hand for a portable x-ray , avoiding immobilisation through anaesthesia -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . General husbandry techniques have also been improved and modified through the use of PRT . In the past , weighing required immobilisation or capture and separation from conspecifics -LRB- McKinley et al. , 2003 -RRB- . An operant conditioning program at the Bronx zoo trained a variety of Neo-tropical primates to station themselves on scales that were temporarily placed in their enclosure -LRB- Savastano et al. 2003 -RRB- . McKinley et al. -LRB- 2003 -RRB- trained common marmosets to climb onto a scale placed in their home cage and remain stationed until their weight was obtained . PRT has also been of value for monitoring pregnancies and other issues regarding maternal care . Keepers at DAS have trained primates to accept ultrasounds -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . A white-cheeked gibbon at DAS was trained in nipple desensitisation and genital presentation to facilitate the monitoring of her pregnancy -LRB- Richards et al. , 2001 -RRB- . Bonobos at Milwaukee County Zoo accept 45-minute ultrasound examinations and one individual allows the use of a breast pump -LRB- cited in Rodgers , 1997 -RRB- . PRT can also reduce the need for hand rearing , even with inexperienced mothers , and aid in early reintroductions in cases where initial hand rearing is necessary . The white-cheeked gibbon at DAS showed evidence of improvement in her maternal behaviour , but the infant had to be removed after 25 days -LRB- Richards et al. , 2001 -RRB- . However , PRT allowed keepers to train the mother and older sibling to feed cooperatively and ignore the presence of a baby bottle . PRT was also used to train the infant to come to the mesh for feedings when a bell sounded . The success of this training allowed the infant to be reintroduced at 4.5 months of age -LRB- Richards et al. , 2001 -RRB- . Infant care training is recognised as a priority at DAS , focusing on mothers who have either a poor history of maternal care , or who are first time mothers -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . Techniques such as training the female to retrieve an object and bring it to the front of the enclosure , can allow an infant to stay within its social group , even when the female is unable to feed her infant -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . Many of the examples outlined above involve a technique known as ` refinement ' in which a procedure that is already being carried out within animal care institutions is modified -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . However , PRT techniques can also be employed to tackle a range of complex problems that without its use might remain unsolved . One such example is behaviour modification -LRB- Schapiro et al. , 2001 ; Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- . This is a particularly relevant term with regard to primates , who often live in complex social groupings . Social dynamics can be problematic within the confines of a captive situation -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- . Bloomsmith et al. -LRB- 1994 -RRB- used PRT techniques to tackle the problem of excessive aggression during feeding time in a group of chimpanzees . The dominant male was very aggressive and often gave chase to subordinate individuals . For the exclusion/reduction of undesirable behaviours , PRT is used to reinforce a behaviour that is incompatible with the undesired behaviour -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- . In this case , the male chimpanzee was trained to station while the other members of the group were fed -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- . Schapiro et al. -LRB- 2001 -RRB- demonstrated how affiliative behaviours within socially housed groups of rhesus macaques could be manipulated through the use of PRT . Female rhesus macaques were divided into groups of high and low affiliators . High affiliators were reinforced for stationing at targets ; these targets were set up at increasing distances from other group members . Low affiliators were reinforced as their targets were moved closer to another monkey -LRB- Schapiro et al. , 2001 -RRB- . In response to the previous lack of quantitative assessment regarding the value of operant conditioning , recent work has attempted to document time-investment and effectiveness . A symposium at the Nineteenth Congress of the International Primatological Society -LRB- China , 2002 -RRB- , was organised to bring together individuals involved in operant conditioning in both zoo and laboratory contexts -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . Following the conference , a special issue of the Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science was published -LRB- Prescott , 2004 -RRB- . Articles focused on quantifying the costs and benefits of training and providing guidelines for application -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 ; Schapiro et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Thus , many of the articles outlined above provided information regarding time investment and level of success . For example , the number of training sessions required for rhesus macaques to cooperate in blood collection varied from 2-27 sessions ; a mean time-investment of 38.5 minutes -LRB- Reinhardt , 2003 -RRB- . Only one member of the animal care staff is needed for this technique , whereas traditional restraint methods required two individuals -LRB- Reinhardt , 2003 -RRB- . Training the chimpanzee to sit during feeding times took one week for him to sit reliably on command ; and after three weeks he remained seated even when preferred food items were fed -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- . Behavioural data showed that antagonistic behaviours -LRB- defined as displays , submission , aggression and reconciliation -RRB- were significantly lower after the training had been implemented -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- . McKinley et al. -LRB- 2003 -RRB- , document a mean time investment of an hour and four minutes per pair of common marmosets for target training and a mean of 52 minutes per pair for urine training . No sex differences were observed during McKinley 's study . However , Bloomsmith et al. -LRB- 1998 -RRB- document significant sex differences regarding training chimpanzees to move from their outdoor to indoor enclosure on request . A mean of 16.1 training sessions were required for all chimps to reach reliable performance levels -LRB- defined by the authors as complying with 90 % of requests -RRB- . However , the female chimpanzees required significantly fewer training sessions than the males . The training programme at the Bronx Zoo discovered significant species differences in response to a range of training procedures . Shyness made golden lion tamarins -LRB- Leontopithecus rosalia -RRB- slow to respond to training . Other tamarin species responded more quickly than the marmosets , but required frequent , short sessions , as they tended to lose interest during longer sessions . Common marmosets were slower to respond to early training sessions - 60 sessions to hand-feed - but subsequent behaviours were learned very quickly , often in less than 10 sessions -LRB- Savastano et al. 2003 -RRB- . One major benefit of PRT is that it negates the need for invasive and stressful techniques , such as darting , capture , restraint and separation from conspecifics -LRB- as outlined above -RRB- . In addition , PRT also facilitates health monitoring -LRB- Schapiro et al. , 2003 -RRB- , gives captive animals more choice and control over daily events -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1998 -RRB- , mitigates social problems -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 ; Schapiro et al. , 2001 -RRB- , and reduces abnormal behaviours -LRB- Laule et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Although animal welfare benefits are the priority , there are other notable positive outcomes of PRT . These include : improved reliability and quantity of data collection -LRB- McKinley et al. , 2003 ; Reinhardt , 2003 -RRB- , positive attitude changes among staff -LRB- Savastano et al. , 2003 -RRB- and positive experiences for zoo visitors , who see visible , interactive animals while learning the importance of low-stress animal husbandry -LRB- Savastano et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Potential costs involved with PRT require exploration . Many researchers show that the initial cost in terms of time investment is recouped within a short period of time -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 ; Reinhardt , 2003 ; Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . In addition , once the first behaviour is trained , animals respond quickly to subsequent training -LRB- Savastano et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Potential animal welfare costs include physical problems from performing tasks too frequently . For example , excessive scent marking could lead to urinary tract infections in marmosets -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . Moreover , scent marking has been associated with stress in marmosets -LRB- Bassett et al. , 2003 -RRB- , highlighting the issue of rewarding a stress-related behaviour -LRB- McKinley et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Saliva samples may prove a viable alternative to urine samples in the future , which might solve such issues -LRB- McKinley et al. , 2003 -RRB- . Within laboratory environments , training should not be seen as a substitute for housing of a high standard and enriching environments , as this could compromise welfare -LRB- Scott et al. , 2003 ; Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . There are also concerns around habituation -LRB- Prescott , pers . comm . -RRB- . However , exposing primates to positive human interactions may increase welfare , especially when frequent human contact is necessary -LRB- Bassett et al. , 2003 -RRB- . O'Sullivan -LRB- 1999 -RRB- explores the issue of training in relation to re-introductions . In this context , PRT can have both positive and negative effects on an animal 's behavioural repertoire . Increased human contact results in animals losing their fear of humans , which could be dangerous in a post-release environment -LRB- O'Sullivan , 1999 -RRB- . However , training can be used to teach skills and appropriate reactions necessary for survival in the wild -LRB- O'Sullivan , 1999 -RRB- . Institutions embarking on PRT programmes must plan to equip keepers with the necessary skills -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- . Consistency is important ; otherwise the wrong behaviours can be accidentally reinforced -LRB- Prescott , pers . comm . -RRB- . Changes in behaviour can occur outside of training -LRB- Prescott , pers . comm . -RRB- . Schapiro et al. -LRB- 2003 -RRB- demonstrated that training altered the overall behavioural repertoire of the low affiliators in their macaque study , which was a positive outcome as affiliation has been linked to psychological well being -LRB- Schapiro et al. , 2001 -RRB- . The chimpanzee trained to station during feeding -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- retained normal levels of aggression and dominance outside the training sessions -LRB- Bloomsmith et al. , 1994 -RRB- , so that the overall hierarchy of the group was not disrupted . These examples illustrate the importance of quantitative data to ensure that training objectives are carefully monitored and ultimately met . This essay has outlined the various ways in which PRT techniques can play a valuable role in the care and management of primates in captivity . Recent attempts to quantify its value in terms of time-investment and its successfulness have helped to clarify this . As demonstrated , some of the more complex potential costs are yet to be properly assessed and the advantages and disadvantages involved deserve more extensive and scientific exploration -LRB- Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . These may vary between species and even individuals , as some studies show significant differences in responses to training . However , these factors should not be used as an excuse for institutions not to implement PRT programmes for standard techniques that are tried and tested , as these present viable alternatives to procedures which are necessary but which traditionally involved handling , restraint and solitary confinement - stressful experiences for the primates involved . There are very few institutions in which PRT would not be useful and beneficial to both the primates and the animal care staff -LRB- Schapiro et al. , 2003 ; Prescott and Buchanan-Smith , 2003 -RRB- . Moreover , institutions that have incorporated PRT into their management practices have found it an indispensable tool -LRB- Colahan and Breder , 2003 -RRB- .
The ability to census primate populations is a vital conservation tool . Primates that occur at low densities are of particular conservation concern , but low densities and cryptic behaviours make some primates difficult to census -LRB- Sutherland , 2000 -RRB- . It is thus imperative to develop the right census method prior to data collection ; these methods are often species and site specific . This essay will outline four different techniques for four different primate species and attempt to compare and contrast methods used . The first study surveyed Alouatta pigra at a site in Mexico . A combination of two methods was used : triangulation of dawn and dusk choruses and subsequent follows of the identified groups within the existing 8km trial system . This gave information on group size and composition . 70 % of the survey was spent counting troops on the terrain ; the remaining 30 % was spent conducting triangulations . The second study estimated the population status of Pongo pygmaeus in primary peat swamp forest , Borneo . Researchers used a non-encounter method of estimating orang-utan density from nest counts . Transects spaced 1km apart were cut through the 500 square kilometre study area . Perpendicular distance of nest to transect was noted , and densities were calculated using the software programme , DISTANCE . A number of parameters were added to the equation in an attempt to correct overestimation of density . The third study surveyed Eulemur fulvus in southeastern Madagascar . Authors provided population density estimates for two subspecies . Densities were obtained by dividing the number of animals seen by the total survey area -LRB- total survey length multiplied by transect width -RRB- . Width was calculated using perpendicular distance of transect to group , with a 50 % criterion for falloff distance . Botanical plots were used to assess habitat quality . The fourth study estimated population density of Tarsius spectrum at a site in northern Sulawesi . A combination of two methods was used : quadrat census and a modified form of the fixed-point count . The 1 square kilometre site was divided into 100 one-hectare plots ; 25 plots were selected using random block design and surveyed . A vegetation survey was also conducted . Tarsier groups were located by vocalisations and traced to sleeping sites . Prior to dusk , observers would count the number of individuals leaving each sleeping tree , noting age and sex . Home range size was estimated through radio telemetry devices . Tarsiers were captured and radio collars were fitted . Full night focal follows were conducted on thirteen individuals from five different groups . In all four surveys , researchers gained essential prior knowledge regarding habitat , topography and species-specific behaviours in an attempt to utilize the most appropriate and reliable method available to them . Two of the surveys used one method ; the other two used a combination of two methods - the distinctive calls of Tarsius spectrum and Alouatta pigra allowed researchers to use vocalisation to locate groups . Tarsius calls also aided sex identification . All four methods provided information in addition to density estimates , including home range size , habitat preference , group size and composition and vegetation quality . The Pongo pygmaeus survey used the only non-encounter method -LRB- nest counts -RRB- , and was the only study using the transect method which cut trails , the other two using existing ones . This was an advantage because it allowed researchers to sample the three principal forest sub-types in the area . Correction factors were required to avoid overestimation of density . Nest density -LRB- divided by proportion of nest makers in the population , multiplied by rate at which nests were produced , multiplied by time a nest remains visible -RRB- equalled orang-utan density . Unfortunately , these parameters were not site/study specific . This meant that their density estimates had a huge range , because they analysed data based on the maximum and minimum range of published values for each parameter , for each habitat type . However , the authors estimated a mid range density of 5671-8951 , based on the knowledge of parameters at their study site . They also point out that more than 2000 individuals is a self-sustaining , viable orang-utan population . Moreover , this method allows a rapid survey of a very large area , without attempting the long process of habituation . The other three surveys covered much smaller areas . The tarsier 's small body size allowed researchers to sample 25 % of the study area -LRB- 39 individuals multiplied by four , equalling density of 156 individuals within one kilometre square -RRB- , producing accurate densities and information about home range and group composition . Similarly , the howlers were followed , repeatedly counted and age/sex noted . The results of the howler survey were stated as preliminary , although their density estimates did compare with other sites . The lemur survey was the shortest in duration , and the least convincing in terms of accuracy . Non-linear , existing transects were used due to time constraints and damage limitation , but authors confessed that densities might have been underestimated due to disturbance , causing cryptic response behaviours . However , they did conduct a test survey at a site where densities were known , and achieved similar results . Assessing the validity and reliability of rapid census methods is important , as time constraints are common . The three smaller surveys multiplied up from the sample results to achieve density estimates , whereas the orang-utan survey offers a density estimate range , based on correction factors . For any one study , it is difficult to determine accuracy . Only through continued monitoring of primate populations does accuracy , validity and reliability increase . Census techniques are required for assessing the conservation status at the initial phase . Baseline data on density is needed for the development of conservation strategies . Authors of all four articles call for the need for follow up studies . Through repeated use , census methods are refined to incorporate correction factors and then the statistical analysis of variance can assess the significance of the data . The development of non-encounter techniques is useful for large areas and non-habituated individuals . In this sense , the orang-utan survey may not be entirely accurate , but it does give a vital density estimate of a large orang-utan population , highlighting the importance of peat swamp forest type in terms of orang-utan conservation -LRB- Morrogh-Bernard , 2000 -RRB- . The tarsier study emphasises the usefulness of trapping techniques for accurate density estimates , home range size and information about group composition , -LRB- although this technique is clearly only possible for smaller primates -RRB- . Vocal primates allow for triangulation , which can be useful for locating groups and/or identification . The lemur survey presents preliminary data . However , it does attempt a comparison between two threatened subspecies of brown lemur , concluding that Eulemur fulvus albocollaris occurs at very low densities in areas surveyed , a result of forest fragmentation and loss . In each of the four articles consulted , researchers made clear attempts to select the method that was best suited to their study site and the species in question , either through the combination of two methods or by the modification of an existing method .
It has long been recognised that the ever expanding human population , and the destructive way in which we utilize the planet 's resources are having a negative impact on wildlife across the globe . Of great concern is the destruction of the world 's tropical forests . This is of particular relevance to non-human primates , as approximately 90 % of all primate species are restricted to tropical forest habitats . It has been demonstrated that human population growth is linked to global deforestation and that ` human population pressure is the best predictor of tropical deforestation rates ' . However , there are non-anthropogenic factors that influence non-human primate distribution and abundance , and these natural species characteristics are important too . This essay will therefore focus on natural determinants of primate distribution on a global scale . It will examine both patterns of distribution and the processes that may be responsible for those patterns . Primates are largely restricted to Central and South America , Sub-Saharan Africa , Madagascar and Southern Asia -LRB- Fleagle , 1999 -RRB- . Geographical ranges of primate species tend to show a lognormal distribution ; in other words , more species have small ranges than large ranges . Cowlishaw and Dunbar -LRB- 2000 -RRB- classify a species as rare when their distribution falls below 170,000 square kilometres . This follows Gaston -LRB- 1994 -RRB- who defines a species as rare in relation to the rest of its taxonomic group . In this way rarity is defined as a relative property . This might be instructive as it could illuminate processes operating at different spatial scales . Similarly , Dobsonal . -LRB- 1995 -RRB- , explain that methods , which aid identification of species that may be at risk , are vital conservation strategies and that assessing relative rarity is one of those methods . Rare species tend to be concentrated on islands and within equatorial regions . Cowlishaw and Dunbar -LRB- 2000 -RRB- point out that in only two of the nine major primate groups are most species rare . These are the tarsiers of Southeast Asia and the lemurs of Madagascar -LRB- two groups largely restricted to islands -RRB- . The tendency for species near to the equator to have small ranges will be discussed later , in relation to ` Rapoport 's rule ' . It is also worth noting that the terms ` rarity ' and ` endemic ' are not always corresponding characteristics . Although islands tend to have endemic species , there are also country and regional endemics , and these species are not always rare . For example , the common marmoset -LRB- Callithrix jacchus -RRB- has a relatively large distribution , but yet it is only found in Brazil -LRB- Wolfheim 1983 -RRB- . Wolfheim attempted a comprehensive guide to the status of all known primates , looking at factors such as distribution , abundance , and ecological flexibility/specialisation . Wolfheim highlights the relationship between island species and restricted geographic ranges . Of the 34 species that she identified as having a tiny range -LRB- smaller than 100,000 square kilometres -RRB- , 24 of these species were island forms . Islands that contain primate species are often those islands that were , until relatively recently , joined to the mainland . For example , in Southeast Asia , the islands of Sumatra , Java , Borneo and Sulawesi , which were connected up until approximately 10,000 years ago , accommodate primate species , whereas New Guinea , which has been isolated for much longer , does not . Rapoport 's rule -LRB- as explained by Cowlishaw and Dunbar , 2000 -RRB- examines whether there is a relationship between species range size and proximity to the equator . In general , species range size increases with latitude . Rapoport 's rule is a common pattern for terrestrial taxa and for South American primates . However , interestingly , African primates only obey this rule consistently when they exist south of the equator . Cowlishaw and Hacker -LRB- 1997 -RRB- examined the relationship between distribution and latitude in African primates in some detail . They conclude that geographical ranges of African primates are actually correlated with their ` exposure to climatic variation ' and that the relationship between range and latitude , described by Rapoport 's rule , is not an inevitable outcome of the relationship between range and climate . In short , Cowlishaw and Hacker acknowledge that primate range size increases with latitude north of the equator , but it increases with the seasonality of rainfall both north and south of the equator . Range size is also affected by a variety of intrinsic and extrinsic factors . Intrinsic factors include body size and ecological flexibility . Extrinsic factors include interspecific interactions and dispersal ability - often limited by constraints such as geographical barriers . Over a period of time , habitats can be subject to modification . For primates , and indeed other species , this may mean the modification of a habitat from suitable to unsuitable . Specialised species may be particularly at risk , because of their lack of ability to adapt . Species that exploit a variety of resources are likely to be both more abundant and more widespread . Dunbar and Barrett -LRB- 2000 -RRB- highlight the genus Macaca as having a particularly broad ranging distribution . Their range stretches from the mountains of Morocco to the temperate forests of Japan . The Barbary macaque is the only non-human primate found in Europe and the Japanese macaque is the most northern primate , adapted for life in harsh , snowy winters . Macaques spend time both in the trees and on the ground and have an omnivorous diet . In contrast , a species like the gelada baboon -LRB- Theropithecus gelada -RRB- is highly specialised . Their diet is restricted to the highlands of Ethiopia and they can not cope with grasses growing below approximately 1,500 metres as they are too fibrous . Ecological specialisation may also indicate the gradual decline in density towards the borders of distribution . Another potentially important intrinsic factor is body size , and the possible link between body size and distribution can be illustrated in three ways . The first is that larger taxa may have higher energy requirements and therefore have populations with low densities and wide distributions . However , this fails to explain the correlation with climatic variability . Many studies show that species have larger distributions where rainfall is more seasonal . This supports the second possible link between body size and distribution , niche breadth . In simple terms , this refers to the idea that larger primates have greater ranges because they tend to be ecological generalists . In addition , larger species may be able to cope better with fluctuations with regard to food availability . The third factor explaining the apparent link between body size and distribution supposes that body size is not the causal factor at all , but rather that it is an indirect measure of another life history trait . One possible example is reproductive rates -LRB- Cowlishaw and Dunbar , 2000 -RRB- . The most important constraint on possible expansion is barriers . Island taxa are particularly affected here ; they can not extend their range because an impassable barrier , the ocean , surrounds them . However , there are other types of barriers . Major river systems , for example , may affect the dispersal ability of some species . However , the effectiveness of a river as a barrier depends on its geographical location , its size and its flow . Rivers may particularly affect small and/or forest-dwelling species . Ayres and Clutton-Brock -LRB- 1992 -RRB- confirm that ` rivers play an important role in constraining the dispersal of Amazonian primates ' . They also found that body weight is positively correlated with geographical range size and that larger primates in the Amazon have fewer subspecies relative to their range size than smaller primates . They explain that this is because the larger species are better able to cross rivers and other environmental barriers . Colyn -LRB- 1988 -RRB- concludes from his study of primates in the Central Zaire Basin that the dense network of large river systems plays an important part in the distribution patterns of guenons . Examples of other types of barriers include the topography of the land and changes in vegetation . As mentioned previously , most primates are limited to tropical forests and therefore boundaries between forest and savannah form particularly important barriers . Whatever the type of barrier , it normally forms the edge of a taxon 's range , thus restricting it and limiting its distribution . Barriers either limit the spread of a species from its evolutionary centre of origin , or split a species range , eventually causing allopatric speciation to occur . Another potentially important extrinsic factor is interspecific interactions . In fact , very little is known about the ways in which interspecific competition might affect primate distribution . However , Cowlishaw and Dunbar -LRB- 2000 -RRB- identify three ways in which this dynamic may be significant in some cases . These are : smaller primates being excluded from food resources by larger primates , closely related taxa with similar ecological requirements competing for the same resource , and the presence of predators and parasites . M.D Pagel et al -LRB- 1991 -RRB- discuss geographical distribution in relation to mammalian species and explain that species ranges are not only likely to be affected by parasites , but moreover by parasites that they share with other species . They suggest that species with broad ranges displace species with narrow ranges when a parasite common to both has a varying impact on the two species and conclude that ` the effects of parasites may be greatest in areas of high density and small range sizes ' . As Cowlishaw and Dunbar -LRB- 2000 -RRB- explain , both range -LRB- distribution -RRB- and abundance -LRB- population density -RRB- are good indicators of the health of a species . These two factors are interlinked because if a species has both a restricted range and low population numbers , then we are likely to refer to that species as rare and possibly vulnerable to extinction . Geographic distribution and population abundance are good indicators of global population size and therefore global extinction risk . In terms of an actual casual link between distribution and abundance , the relationship has been described as triangular rather than strictly linear . In simple terms , while species that have large ranges often also occur at low densities , species with small geographical ranges occur at both high and low densities . This essay has attempted to show that there are a number of broad-scale , biogeographical patterns in relation to primate distribution . Eeley and Lawes -LRB- 1999 -RRB- examine these patterns in the continents of South America and Africa and confirm that they are consistent for both continents . In summary , species range size appears to increase with latitude , although this may be linked to climatic variability -LRB- particularly in Africa , see above -RRB- ; ecological generalists tend to have broader ranges than specialists and larger species tend to be widespread , although small species can have small or large ranges . More primates have small ranges than large ranges and most primates are forest dwelling . Small range size is associated with forest biome , and the few species that inhabit savannah or woodland habitats tend to be more widespread . Primates of Southeast Asia and Madagascar follow different general patterns , and are dominated by island effects . As illustrated by Wolfheim -LRB- 1983 -RRB- , many island primates have restricted ranges . Island primates have impassable boundaries and consequent limited opportunities for dispersal . The somewhat weak correlation between abundance and distribution , and the fact that the majority of primates occupy small ranges clearly has implications for conservation . Pagel et al -LRB- 1991 -RRB- show that a relatively small environmental change may affect and threaten a relatively large number of species . As explained , primates are associated with tropical forests and this is a habitat that is under severe pressure from activities such as logging , agriculture and plantations , mining , and hunting . Moreover , forest cover is linked with species richness and endemic species . Wolfheim 's -LRB- 1983 -RRB- comprehensive and ambitious study shows that both anthropogenic and natural characteristics influence the status of primate populations . Wolfheim refers to natural characteristics as ultimate causes , which include density , habitat , body size and original geographic range . She refers to anthropogenic factors as proximate causes . In the absence of human action , proximate factors would include spontaneous events such as climate change . However , human activities are now overshadowing natural processes , and thus Wolfheim refers solely to habitat alteration and human predation in relation to proximate factors . Both proximate and ultimate factors work together in a precarious balancing act that tips species toward either survival or extinction . This essay has attempted to outline patterns of distribution and to discuss the processes that are responsible for these patterns . It is essential that we understand these patterns and processes , because , as Wolfheim explains , geographical range is the most important ultimate cause of the status of primate populations . Moreover , other ultimate causes identified by Wolfheim are degree of ecological specialisation , abundance and body size , factors that interact in various ways with range size . This essay has demonstrated that highly specialised species tend towards restricted ranges so that habitat alteration and/or predation can rapidly tip the balance towards extinction . Larger primates face similar risks , because although they may have relatively larger ranges they often also occur at low densities . What is clear is that distribution and abundance are complex and important issues , which need to be carefully assessed in the development of conservation strategies , aimed at saving primate species at risk .
While it was once believed that primate social systems were ` closed genetic units ' , it has now been documented that there is considerable movement of individuals between groups -LRB- Pusey and Packer , 1987 -RRB- . This essay will outline different dispersal strategies and discuss the various hypotheses that have been put forward to explain why primates disperse . It will focus specifically on three different examples of dispersal types , female-biased dispersal in chimpanzees -LRB- Pan troglodytes -RRB- , male-biased dispersal in East African vervet monkeys -LRB- Cercopithecus aethiops -RRB- and both-sex dispersal in red howler monkeys -LRB- Alouatta seniculus -RRB- , but touch on other examples where relevant . These strategies will then be discussed in an attempt to clarify the genetic consequences of dispersal for the individual , the social group and species population structure . Implications for conservation will also be discussed . Although it was previously assumed that male-biased dispersal was the norm , there is growing evidence to show that many species practice bisexual dispersal and also a few species where females disperse . Species in which the females are philopatric and the males disperse are probably still the most common , certainly with most cercopithecines -LRB- Cheney and Seyfarth , 1983 -RRB- . However , many lemurs , some colobines and almost all neotropical primates provide examples of species where both sexes disperse - although one sex may travel further than the other -LRB- Cowlishaw and Dunbar , 2000 -RRB- . Situations also occur where the males are philopatric and the females disperse , and this is true of chimpanzees , muriquis , spider monkeys and gorillas -LRB- Pusey and Packer , 1987 , Cowlishaw and Dunbar , 2000 -RRB- . The timing of dispersal is often puberty for males and after sexual cycling has commenced for females -LRB- Pusey and Packer , 1987 -RRB- . In the past , it was assumed that primates disperse as an inbreeding avoidance strategy -LRB- Moore and Ali , 1984 , Cheney and Seyfarth , 1983 , Smith , 1992 -RRB- . However , more recently the reasons for dispersal have become a topic of scientific debate . Several hypotheses have been put forward including intrasexual competition for resources - both food and mates -LRB- Moore and Ali , 1984 , Sterck , 1997 -RRB- , territory choice -LRB- Moore and Ali , 1984 -RRB- , avoidance of infanticide in combination with mate choice -LRB- Sterck , 1997 , Pusey and Packer , 1987 -RRB- , attraction of novel mating partners -LRB- Smith , 1992 -RRB- and abduction - of females by males , only common in hamadryas baboons -LRB- Pusey and Packer , 1984 -RRB- . Certain events coinciding with the timing of dispersal appear to support different hypotheses as to the motivations behind dispersal . For example , Pusey -LRB- 1980 -RRB- explains that the timing of female dispersal in chimpanzees which generally occurs during oestrus periods and the fact that the females often return to their natal groups either between oestrus periods or once they have become pregnant , strongly supports the inbreeding avoidance hypothesis . Sterck -LRB- 1997 -RRB- describes female-biased dispersal in Thomas langurs -LRB- Presbytis thomasi -RRB- and concludes that the proximate cause is infanticide avoidance while the ultimate cause is likely to be inbreeding avoidance . Again , the timing is key , as females tend to transfer when the dominant male of their group is no longer a good protector . Moore and Ali -LRB- 1984 -RRB- explain that there are a number of costs and benefits involved with any dispersal strategy . Benefits include inbreeding avoidance , habitat choice , mate availability and the avoidance of intraspecific competition . Costs include potentially increased risks of predation , disease and exposure and the danger of being attacked by the conspecifics of the new group . Primates are faced with weighing up the costs and benefits of dispersal and often include other inbreeding avoidance strategies including kin-recognition -LRB- Moore and Ali , 1984 -RRB- and preference for unfamiliar mates -LRB- Starin , 2001 -RRB- . Whether inbreeding avoidance is a proximate or ultimate cause of dispersal strategies , these tactics still have genetic implications , which work at many levels . In order to explain this further , it is necessary to explore different primate social systems and the dispersal strategies that operate within these systems in some detail . Pusey -LRB- 1980 -RRB- collected data on a habituated group of chimpanzees at Gombe National Park , Tanzania . Chimpanzees are known to operate within a fission-fusion social system - a wider community of males , females and offspring who split up to travel or feed alone or in ` parties ' , consisting of any combination of individuals belonging to the community -LRB- Dunbar and Barrett , 2000 -RRB- . As mentioned earlier , male chimpanzees are philopatric , while the females disperse . Pusey documented that as females begin sexual cycling their association with ` pre-oestrus favourite males ' -LRB- often maternal relatives -RRB- decreases . This behaviour also coincides with shifts in range use and visits to other communities . Eventually , females either transfer permanently to other communities , or transfer temporarily while in oestrus , returning to their natal groups either between cycles or once they become pregnant . Females are not evicted from natal groups , but go by choice , despite the fact that they often experience extreme hostility from the resident females of the new group -LRB- Pusey , 1980 -RRB- . In contrast , East African vervet monkeys operate a system of male-biased dispersal . Cheney and Seyfarth -LRB- 1983 -RRB- conducted research on a population of free-ranging vervets in Amboseli National Park , Kenya . Vervets live in multi-male groups of 12-27 individuals . The females always remain in their natal groups while the males transfer to neighbouring groups when they reach sexual maturity . Male vervet dispersal patterns can be defined as ` non-random ' ; 88 % of males transferred to adjacent groups , which included old members of their previous group . Moreover , males tended to transfer with brothers . However , random transfer also occurs . This is common in older males who are transferring for the second time . Such males tend to transfer alone and cover larger distances . This is likely to be because the older males are bigger and better able to defend themselves . These males may also incur genetic costs if they transfer back into their natal group -LRB- Cheney and Seyfarth , 1983 -RRB- . An example of both-sex dispersal is offered through an examination of red howler monkeys . Pope -LRB- 1992 -RRB- studied two populations of red howler monkeys in Venezuela . One population was living to the west of the area on a cattle ranch -LRB- the woodland community -RRB- ; the other population was living to the east on a wildlife reserve -LRB- the gallery forest community -RRB- . The two communities are considered to be two distinct populations , with some unknown amount of gene flow taking place between them -LRB- Pope , 1992 -RRB- . Red howlers form multi-male troops , although one alfa-male fathers all or most offspring -LRB- a harem -RRB- . Most males left their natal groups before adulthood -LRB- 94 % -RRB- , joining other groups . Males dispersed several times but did not travel far -LRB- one home range diameter -RRB- . Females also operated high dispersal rates -LRB- 73 % -RRB- , but almost never transferred into existing groups , instead founding new troops with other solitary migrants . Females travelled much further than males -LRB- 6 home range diameters -RRB- . Once a female achieved breeding status within a troop , she remained there throughout her reproductive lifespan . The social systems described above reveal much about the varying genetic consequences of dispersal . First , let us consider the individual . We can assume that it is in the interests of all individuals to produce fit , viable offspring to ensure that their genes are represented in the next generation . Within chimpanzee communities , the responsibility of inbreeding avoidance is on the females , who must avoid copulation with ` pre-oestrus ' favourite but maternally related males and seek unfamiliar partners in new groups despite aggression from resident females . Mating between close kin is thought to cause an increase in homozygosity and the possible emergence of deleterious , recessive alleles -LRB- Smith , 1992 -RRB- . However , fraternal and peer migration , which has not only been documented for vervets but also rhesus macaques , Japanese macaques and baboons -LRB- Cheney and Seyfarth , 1983 -RRB- , can be viewed as a balance of the costs and benefits of dispersal . It raises the level of genetic relatedness within and between groups but offers the individual support during aggressive interactions associated with migration , therefore ensuring their survival . In addition , mating with individuals who share some degree of relatedness may carry some selective advantage -LRB- Cheney and Seyfarth , 1983 -RRB- . Dispersal patterns affect levels of genetic variation within and among primate social groups . Again , different patterns have different consequences . In chimpanzee societies , although the males remain within their natal group and the responsibility is on the female to disperse , the males do play a role . Potential attacks on new females by resident females often prompt intervention by the resident males , who threaten the resident females and stay close to the new females , often copulating with them and thus ensuring the union of genetically unrelated individuals -LRB- Pusey , 1980 -RRB- . As the males are closely related , they have a collective interest in the health of the group and its continued survival . Within red howler populations , gene flow among troops was exclusively through the exchange of breeding males -LRB- on average troop leaders were replaced once every 7.5 years -LRB- Pope , 1992 -RRB- -RRB- . However , the breeding females represented the highest level of genetic variation among troops . Unusually , new red howler troops are not formed through fissioning of larger groups along matrilineal lines , but rather by immigrant females . Therefore , females are not related . Unrelated males mate within genetically divergent matrilines , which increases the variance in gene frequencies among troops but decreases the correlation between uniting gametes within troops -LRB- Pope , 1992 -RRB- . As vervet females are philopatric , the length of tenure of a male in any one group is likely to be set by the sexual maturity of potential daughters . Therefore , males must transfer twice , possibly more . Within non-random , peer migrations , brothers not only have alliances in their new social group , but can also keep track of close kin -LRB- i.e. brother 's daughters , which need to be avoided as mating partners -LRB- Cheney and Seyfarth , 1983 -RRB- -RRB- . The question of random versus non-random dispersal within vervet populations can also be discussed in terms of the species population structure , as the size of the ` deme ' -LRB- the interbreeding population -RRB- is hugely affected by the level of migration between groups -LRB- Cowlishaw and Dunbar , 2000 -RRB- . Random dispersal would result in the most diverse migrant pool and would also serve to maximise effective population size . In a population of eight groups , random dispersal would create a population four times larger than non-random dispersal -LRB- Cheney and Seyfarth , 1992 -RRB- . In contrast , non-random dispersal would result in the least diverse migrant pool and minimise effective population size . Cheney and Seyfarth -LRB- 1992 -RRB- conclude that vervets operate a system of non-random dispersal followed by a secondary random dispersal . This falls in-between simple random versus non-random dispersal , and is a combination of both techniques as a way of maximising the benefits and minimising the costs of dispersal . Knowledge regarding dispersal patterns and the ways in which these patterns help to keep primate populations healthy has important implications for conservation . We need to understand the dispersal tactics of different species as it relates to factors such as viable protected area size and the construction of corridors between fragmented areas . The presence of researchers and the habituation of groups must also be approached with the greatest of caution as it may disrupt social systems . For example , Pusey -LRB- 1980 -RRB- warns that the provisioning of the habituated community of chimpanzees at Gombe may have affected natural female dispersal patterns . Although females from the habituated community did transfer for temporary periods , many females returned and actually mated with males from their natal community -LRB- perhaps drawn back by the temptation of provisions -RRB- . Researchers must ensure that they are documenting normal patterns of inter group transfer -LRB- Pusey , 1980 -RRB- . Knowledge about genetic variation within populations and species can also be useful as heterozygosity can affect adaptability levels -LRB- especially relevant as primates are subject to so many detrimental anthropogenic factors -RRB- . The high levels of heterozygosity and large genetic differences among troops of red howlers may have provided the means by which this species has been able to adapt rapidly to changing habitats -LRB- Pope , 1992 -RRB- , as genetic diversity is required for primate populations to evolve in response to environmental change -LRB- Frankham et al. , 2002 -RRB- . In conclusion , the full range of proximate factors that trigger dispersal are probably yet to be identified -LRB- Smith , 1992 -RRB- . In many species , the tactics , timing and distance travelled during dispersal seem to involve a weighing up of associated costs and benefits . Two common factors of dispersal , that it is highly sex biased and that secondary transfer is common but rarely involves a return to the natal group , seem to support the inbreeding avoidance hypothesis -LRB- Pusey and Packer , 1987 -RRB- . However , whatever the motivation , high levels of dispersal can cause high levels of heterozygosity -LRB- Pusey and Packer , 1987 -RRB- . It is also clear that dispersal affects both the direction and level of gene flow among groups and the level of relatedness of individuals within groups -LRB- Melnick and Hoelzer , 1996 -RRB- . Dispersal strategies must be considered when developing any conservation initiative , for the reasons outlined above .

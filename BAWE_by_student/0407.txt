We all feel as though we are truly interacting in the world . Every day we face a variety of options from which we can freely choose . We buy into the idea that we have a complex process of reasoning and deliberating that can override our natural instinct or change the effect that external influences have upon us . The popular belief is that our action is free , but how can we account for the spontaneity of human freedom in a world follows natural laws of causality ? Determinism holds that human freedom , as we conceive it , would violate natural law . Galen Strawson elucidates ; `` Determinism is the view that the history of the universe is fixed in such a way that nothing can happen otherwise than it does because everything that happens is necessitated by what has already gone before . '' If humans live in a deterministic universe , then our actions may also be fixed by determinate causes . Determinism , as outlined by Simon Blackburn , can be set out as follows : P1 -RRB- The past controls the present and the future . P2 -RRB- You can not control the past P3 -RRB- You can not control the way the past controls the present and the future . C -RRB- Therefore you can not control the present and the future . The idea behind determinism is that any present moment is fixed by what happened immediately before , following the fixed laws of nature . The prima facie implication this has that if determinism exists , free will is not possible . This essay will discuss two fundamental philosophers who both argue that despite the reality of determinism we can have freedom . Hume construes a version of freedom that is compatible with determinism , and his position is traditionally known as the Compatibilist position . Kant initially insists that freedom and determinism are incompatible , however he reaches a similar conclusion to Hume through a complex metaphysical theory . Hume 's account of human freedom -LRB- liberty and necessity -RRB- appears in both his Enquiries Concerning Human Understanding and his Treatise of Human Nature . What Hume was aiming to do in these two works was to create a science of human nature . To do this he had to show that actions and choices possessed the same uniformity as other causes and events do in the physical world . Hume also wanted to show that there is nothing inconsistent in a caused and free action . He calls his account a `` reconciling project '' -LRB- § 8.73 , Enquiries -RRB- and this may refer to a reconciliation between liberty and necessity or a reconciliation between a need for human science and the need for a moral disclosure . Hume 's position on causation is the pivotal axis for his compatibilism , and the section on ` liberty and necessity ' follows on from the section on ` the idea of a necessary connection ' . A necessary connection refers to a sort of force that drives one action to follow another ; `` matter , in all its operation , is actuated by a necessary force , '' -LRB- § 8.64 -RRB- Being an Empiricist , Hume believed that any knowledge not given in experience is unreliable and noted that causation can not ever be experienced . We are confined to saying that we simply observe an effect following on from a cause , but we can not say that we have knowledge of a causal relationship between two events . Hume treats his examination of human nature in the same way as he treats natural events . Hume supposes that human nature fits in perfectly with a law-abiding and predictable world . We can not assert that there is a necessary connection between motives and actions and other human related consequences , just as we can not assert a necessary connection in the physical world , but one must observe that there is a constant conjunction between 'cause s ' and ` effects ' in human nature . If we could not observe a constant conjunction and if nothing were conjoined repeatedly to what went before then we would never have come up with the idea of a necessary connection . John Passmore gives the following example of a baby boy who has only ever been given soft toys to play with . One day the boy 's uncle gives him a rubber ball to play with , which he subsequently drops . The baby has no way of knowing that the ball will bounce , unlike his other toys which quietly thud to the floor . What the baby lacks and the uncle possesses is simply experience . The uncle has an idea , using Hume 's terminology , of a constant conjunction between the rubber ball being dropped and it 's bouncing , and can infer from one instance to another . The two conditions of necessity then , for Hume , are conjunction and inference ; `` Beyond the constant conjunction of similar objects , and the subsequent inference from one to the other , we have no notion of any necessity or connexion . '' -LRB- § 8.64 , Enquiries -RRB- The uniformity present in nature provides us with an idea of necessity and causation , by way of an internal impression coming directly from repeated conjunctions of A and B , from which the idea of the necessary connection of A and B is derived . For Hume this distinction between impression and idea is important . Impressions are what we gain through perception and experience , they are lively and forceful , whereas ideas are what we have in thought and these are less lively and forceful . We have no impression of the necessary connection , only an idea , and this means we have no direct knowledge of it ; `` Our idea , therefore , of necessity and causation arises entirely from the uniformity observable in the operations of nature , '' -LRB- § 8.64 , Enquiries , italics added -RRB- Hume accuses the rationalist position of holding that ` if we ca n't know it , it is not the case ' , but Hume says there are mechanisms that force things to happen and we just can not see behind the regularity to the inner workings . Hume begins his section Of Liberty and Necessity with a close description of the way in which language and definitions pose an obstacle for the problem of free will ; `` the whole controversy has hitherto turned merely upon words . '' -LRB- § 8.63 , Enquiries -RRB- Hume assures us that since everyone shares the same views on the doctrines of liberty and necessity , the entire issue relies ultimately upon an uncertain definition of the terms ` freedom ' and ` necessity ' . At this point that Hume assures his readers that his argument will renew their interest in the subject . Hume applies this theory of the natural world to human nature using the support of his studies in history . He suggests that in mankind we can observe a certain uniformity . The same motives tend to produce the same actions , to the extent that we can largely predict people 's behaviour and reactions ; `` there is a great uniformity among the actions of men , '' -LRB- § 8.65 -RRB- Hume is not suggesting that all men will react in the same way to the same situation , and our predictions are of course made more specific if we know the characteristics and tendencies of the particular person . The passions are the source of all actions , and he uses examples from the uniformity observed in the human nature of Greeks and Romans , French and English and so on -LRB- although this would be questioned by come contemporary historians -RRB- . Common intuition would suggest that men do act in less than uniform ways , and sometimes with irregularity , but Hume counters that the `` human body is a mighty complicated machine '' -LRB- § 8.67 , Enquiries -RRB- . The irregular events may just be a product of internal , unobservable operations and can not be attributed to human spontaneity . Irregular resolutions of men may be due to chance , such as a good tempered man flying into a rage when he has a fever or is in great pain . This irregularity itself is a feature of nature . Hume uses the example of a peasant who can not tell why a clock does no work other than stating it is broken , whereas an artist has an insight into the inner workings and can tell the specific cause of the problem , perhaps alluding to his own insight as a philosopher . The same conjunction can be observed between motive and action as between cause and effect , and Hume posits that this has been universally accepted . From this Hume reasons that we also observe the same conjunction and expect uniformity between men 's characters and their conduct . We can apply inference to our account of human necessity since we rely on inferences every day . Every action we perform is performed with reflection on the actions of others ; `` The mutual dependence of men is so great in all societies that scarce any human action is entirely complete in itself , '' -LRB- § 8 . 69 , Enquiries -RRB- We expect that people will be consistent in their behaviour , according to their characters . There would be no history or politics or morality without inference . Despite Hume 's empirical evidence men insist that they observe a necessary connection in the outside world and it is because they do not feel this connection in their minds they assume that there is a difference between material causality and intelligent causality . But when men speak of liberty they can not surely be suggesting that it has so little to do with motives and inclinations that we observe little conformity in human nature . We need to realise that necessity is present in every aspect of our behaviour , and we should begin by examining the unintelligible processes of the body rather than the ` soul ' or the ` will ' . The idea of necessity has always been accepted by philosophers and Hume presents us with a version of liberty that is compatible with necessity ; `` By liberty , then , we can only mean a power of acting or not acting , according to the determinations of the will . '' -LRB- § 8.73 , Enquiries -RRB- . In fact freedom and determinism just do not clash whatsoever , and are in fact essential features of each other . The conflict is between freedom and constraint , not freedom and determinism . In his Treatise Hume makes a distinction between two types of freedom ; liberty of spontaneity and liberty of indifference . The liberty of spontaneity is freedom from external restraint . This type of freedom is compatible with natural causation and allows us to act as we choose . The liberty of indifference is that we have the freedom to choose to act other than we do act -LRB- Quine calls this a ` sordid boon ' , a wasted effort -RRB- . Popular opinion holds that these powers of unexercised choice are real , and this is the real ` freedom of the will ' that is widely held . Hume attacks the liberty of indifference in three ways : firstly by asserting universal causality , secondly from the pragmatic need to use inference to predict human behaviour and affairs in our day to day lives , and thirdly by refuting that we can introspect natural reality -LRB- thus refuting rationalism -RRB- . The very idea of the liberty of indifference is inconsistent with a science of human nature and morality . Hume asserts that there is no way to step outside of the constant conjunction of cause and effect , and humans will always act as they are inclined to do . This includes a notion of determinism because we choose and act the way we do due to our character , passions and inclinations , the sum of which is due to previous experience or genetic dispositions . We do not spontaneously start new causal chains of events , but we are free because to be free means simply to act out our inclinations , to do as we want to do . This is quite a specific view of freedom , a sort of negative freedom , since one is free unless one is perhaps physically restrained or compelled ; `` this hypothetical liberty is universally allowed to belong to every one who is not a prisoner and in chains . '' -LRB- § 8.73 , Enquiries -RRB- Hume therefore believes that his natural account of determinism does not preclude our freedom , however some may object to Hume here saying that this is not what we meant by freedom at all , we mean freedom of ` the will ' . Those who adamantly cling to a more libertarian notion of freedom will no doubt be more appeased by Kant 's account , however they might not be swayed by the measures Kant employs in order to preserve what Hume would call the ` liberty of indifference ' . Kant intuitively holds an incompatibilist position ; a strong belief in freedom and a strong belief in causality . Of course we would generally expect an incompatibilist to hold one or the other true , and deny the other . Kant feels he can not surrender either and so he actually shares the same aims as the compatibilist . This is the cause of the Third Antinomy . Exactly what position he assumes is rather unclear , and this has led some to suggest that Kant essentially creates a compatibility between Compatibilism and Incompatibilism . This will be discussed later -LRB- his incompatibilist view turns out to be compatible on a higher level ; our actions are determined by our own , uncaused reason . -RRB- Kant 's main discussion of free will forms his 3 rd Antinomy in the Critique of Pure Reason . ` Antinomy ' means paradox , and each side of the paradox has a good argument and a lot of evidence . Antinomies do not strictly concern practical freedom or human affairs , but surround more cosmological issues . It is interesting that Kant chooses to present his concerns about human freedom from this starting point . The reason for this is his eventual conclusion ; the distinction between the intelligible world and the sensible world . Kant aims to show that if we can accept the possibility of transcendental freedom , this allows us the possibility of practical freedom . Transcendental freedom is more of a cosmological issue than a human issue , and is concerned with possibility rather than fact , and this is a stark contrast to Hume 's natural , empirical account . Despite being a rationalist , Kant offers us no certainties . His argument presents us with the conditions required for the possibility of freedom , and Kant explicitly states that he does not think it possible to prove that we are free . Kant assumes , like Hume , that whatever happens has a cause , however he did not think that Hume explained causality , only made it disappear . Causality ? In Kant 's thesis he assumes that it follows whatever an event is -LRB- e -RRB- , it has to have followed a cause -LRB- c -RRB- , this cause also has to have had a cause -LRB- c-1 -RRB- , which requires -LRB- c-2 -RRB- and so on ad infinitum ; `` The causality , therefore , of a cause , whereby something happens , is itself a thing that has happened . '' -LRB- Critique p. 252 -RRB- It would seem natural here for Kant to suggest a problem with infinity , but he is more acutely concerned with the very nature of causality . Kant 's problem is that a ` sufficient cause ' can not be found in conditions which are caused , so it follows that this can not be the only causality . A form of ` absolute spontaneity ' must exist ; `` there must exist an absolute spontaneity of cause , which of itself originates a series of phenomena which proceeds according to natural laws '' -LRB- Critique p. 253 -RRB- Schopenhauer , in his criticism of Kant simply holds that state A is a sufficient cause of state B. Perhaps what Kant then meant by ` sufficient cause ' was 'cause which is sufficiently determined ' . In the solution to the thesis Kant makes it clear that the freedom he is considering is specifically transcendental , and very different from the common psychological use of the word -LRB- which constitutes a merely empirical investigation -RRB- . Transcendental freedom may have nothing to do with human freedom or morality , but we need to support a notion of transcendental freedom to hold practical reason . The ` big bang ' may have been an example of an initial uncaused cause . If it was this would be an example of -LRB- and also prove -RRB- transcendental freedom . This is a purely metaphysical concept that concerns the first cause . He is only concerned with the idea of a spontaneous causal origin . He does not feel the need to account for how we come to have this faculty , since we can not account for natural causality either , but simply accept it a priori ; `` How such a faculty is possible , is not a necessary enquiry ; '' -LRB- Critique p. 254 -RRB- We have to accept that there is a faculty that can of itself originate a series in time -LRB- the first ever action -RRB- , and once we have proved this we can attribute all humans with free action . Despite the fact that Kant feels no real need to prove the reality of ` transcendental ' freedom , he insists that practical freedom presupposes and relies upon transcendental freedom . Kant also holds that we have to accept practical reason because it is essential for morality , and even if we hold practical reason true , this only provides us with a capacity for free action . Kant reasoning here is perhaps not creditable with philosophical merit . Kant is generally impressed by the philosophy of David Hume who awoke him from a ` dogmatic slumber ' , after which he re-evaluated his grounds for believing it was possible to create an unchanging metaphysics . Incidentally Kant was not convinced by Hume 's Compatibilism . The antithesis clearly presents an argument against Hume . Kant is looking for a stronger sense of freedom than Hume . The freedom that Kant thinks is essential excludes causal determinism . Kant meaning of freedom allows humans to originate a new causal series , a sort of spontaneous originality . Freedom is not just the power to choose what one wants or desires -LRB- as Hume held -RRB- , but the power to pursue our moral oughts -LRB- where ` ought ' implies ` can ' -RRB- and to act as a spontaneous first cause of a chain of events . Hume is a defender of the ` all nature ' account that Kant obviously opposes . Hume , in Kant 's words , would argue that if you do not accept that the world has a mathematical first then there is no need to look for a dynamical first . Nature is unlimited and we should not search for a first cause . The substances of the world have always existed and the changes of the conditions of these substances have always existed . Kant supposes the consequences of a naturalist viewpoint ; if we suppose there is a transcendental freedom then nothing can precede to determine a free action . But , Kant says , `` every beginning of action presupposes in the acting cause a state of inaction . '' -LRB- Critique p. 252 -RRB- Transcendental freedom then violates the natural law of cause and effect , and so can not account for a unified experience . Therefore there can be nothing but nature . Nature conforms to law , and freedom conforms to lawlessness . For Kant the possibility of such an infinite derivation is incomprehensible . If the faculty of freedom exists it can not exist as a part of the phenomenal world . Therefore we can not attribute it to objects of the phenomenal world ; `` this faculty must at least exist out of and apart from the world ; '' -LRB- Critique p. 255 -RRB- A system of natural law seems irreconcilable with a lawless faculty of freedom since they would continually conflict and interfere with each other . To solve the antinomy that Kant poses he makes use of a complex theory of metaphysics to explain how , despite the fact that freedom can not be a part of the phenomenal world , it can exist apart from the phenomenal world and create a unified experience . Kant 's holds a position of transcendental idealism . Briefly , this makes a distinction between the phenomenal , sensible world of experience ; they way things appear to us , and the noumenal , intelligible world accessed through reason ; they way things are in themselves . Knowledge conforms to our perception , as opposed to our perception conforming to knowledge of something external , ` out there ' . Space , time and causal order are only features of the sensible world , things as they appear to us . Man 's faculty of reason is a part of the intelligible world , where natural causality plays no role ; `` The positive concept of freedom ... can not , as in the case of physical causes , be the nature of the sensible world . '' -LRB- Ch . III 447 , Groundwork -RRB- There is nothing then restraining the possibility of a free action of reason , or more specifically of the will . Human freedom could have its own causality , one that originates new causal chains of events . This may seem a rather outlandish and perhaps incoherent account of freedom , one that assigns us more autonomy than we are perhaps comfortable with being assigned . Hume 's account certainly appeals through its simplicity . Kant here feels he can reconcile universal causality and a causality of freedom . When we act we are acting as agents within the noumenal realm . In a free human action the motivation -LRB- cause -RRB- and the action are part of the intelligible realm , but this produces a sensible effect . The cause is outside of the causal laws of nature , but we can perceive the regularity and conformity of the effect to the laws of sensible causality . Contrary to the position he assumes in the antithesis , the two sorts of causality do not conflict because they do not meet , since one is empirical and one is rational . Freedom succeeds the determining laws of nature but does not arise form them . Both causal determinism and human freedom are , under this interpretation , compatible . In the Groundwork For the Metaphysics of Morals , Kant clearly employs his account of freedom in his moral theory . He gives a negative account of freedom in terms of what it is from -LRB- not only restraint but natural causation -RRB- . The thesis Hume posits offers no positive account of what freedom is since freedom for Hume is characterised by a lack of constraint ; it is not a positive force like necessity , but simply allows natural causation to take place without interference . Kant does intend to account for what positive freedom is , but merely asserts that it is to act under self-legislating laws and is identical with autonomy ; `` What else then can freedom of the will be but autonomy - that is , the property which will has of being a law to itself ? '' -LRB- Ch.III 447 , Groundwork -RRB- . He explains that we need further preparation if we are to logically infer freedom from practical reason . We must presuppose that freedom is possessed by all rational beings . The extent to which this elucidates his account of positive freedom is questionable . Any account of practical freedom requires an account of morality , since for Kant our desires are controlled by our ` negative freedom ' and our positive freedom comes into actuality only when we are acting morally . `` a free will and a will under moral laws are one and the same '' -LRB- Ch.III 447 Groundwork -RRB- , our autonomy is morality . Henry Sidgwick argues that if only moral action is free , then immoral action must be unfree . Sensuous desires must arise from natural necessity . Does this then entail that we are not responsible for our immoral actions ? The essay will now turn its attention to examining the implications freedom has for morality and moral responsibility . Hume shows how liberty and necessity are not only consistent with each other , but also with morality . Moral responsibility is actually undermined by a lack of determinism because moral responsibility requires ownership of action and this assumes causation . This means that when we act we act intentionally , i.e. in terms of our beliefs and desires and so on . If we can act according to our intentions without constraint , Hume says we are acting freely . We do not need the freedom to act other than we did . Some worry about the consequence this has for morality . If someone could not genuinely have chosen to act other than he did , we have no right to assign praise or blame for his action . If a man is naturally inclined to be a thief , or his environment encourages him to steal , this disposition arises from natural causation . It is only due to genetic disposition or external conditioning . Surely this limits his accountability for his actions . Hume agrees that we make the choices we make because of our attitudes , intuitions and experiences . But rewards and punishments have a regular influence on our minds and subsequently our behaviour . If the thief is blamed and punished , this should be enough to change his character so that he does not steal in the future . `` All laws being founded on rewards and punishments , it is supposed as a fundamental principle , that these motives have a regular and uniform influence on the mind , and both produce the good and prevent the evil actions . '' -LRB- § 8.76 , Enquiries -RRB- The implementation of punishments and rewards would certainly have an effect on our future choices . In this way our decision-making can be conditioned . If the actions men make do not proceed from their character they are not liable for them , and `` men are not blamed for such actions as they perform ignorantly '' -LRB- § 8.76 , Enquiries -RRB- . But this does not make these actions free , only a product of chance or randomness . When people hold the liberty of indifference people are holding that actions are uncaused . If we could ` self-determine ' actions , these actions would presumable follow a uniform pattern , or else be arbitrary . Without liberty -LRB- of spontaneity -RRB- to act or not act without restraint , men are not responsible for their actions . Since we can infer from action to motive we have the grounds on which to praise or blame . Hume 's account does not allow us to praise and blame in order to give someone their just deserts , since we are not the direct cause of our character , passions and affections . We are merely using praise and blame in a pragmatic way , in a way to modify or maintain subsequent behaviour . This seems like a downfall for Hume , but does it really damage his account or is it another ` sordid boon ' , perhaps we are too concerned with a realist notion of justice and should settle for pragmatism . What may be a more crucial issue is that we are surrendering our morality to convention , or the state . The implication Hume makes in this account of morality , is that laws are needed because if people were left to act out of their desires or wishes , society would not function . It follows that the society we have , which issues praise and blame , itself acts to constrain our natural desires . Using Hume 's account it is enough to say that for an action to be someone 's own it has to be caused by someone 's psychological reasons -LRB- his acting according to his desires or beliefs etc. -RRB- . Kant thinks that Hume is too simplistic , and using a modern argument it becomes apparent that Hume can not account for compulsive disorders like kleptomania where the kleptomaniac is doing what he wants but is acting under a compulsive disorder . In this sense Kant liberates us from compulsion as well as constraint . In defence of Hume we could add that the kleptomaniac 's acting under compulsion enough to equal a form a constraint and that , given a rational choice , the sufferer would not want to act in this way . But this example does raise an interesting argument . Perhaps by drawing an empirical cut-off point Hume is missing an intangible , but nonetheless essential human ability . Hume assures us that causality is a property of the states of the world not of substances and we should perhaps be suspicious of Kant 's account and motives . For Kant when we act immorality it is in instances where we lack freedom . This may be due to a self-deception , where a desire convinces reason that a certain act is good and makes us act irrationally , however Kant assumes that we can not make judgments through desires . A judgment is a claim of the form `` I have a reason to do x. . . '' Any desires we have become adapted and transformed into reason . Desire itself plays no causal role in our moral judgments . Kant thinks that those who claim a simple voluntary action is enough to credit moral responsibility perform a ` wretched subterfuge ' . For Kant moral responsibility requires that the agent 's action was caused by his practical reason , and that the agent begins a new series of causality . Hume says the agent does what he wants to do , but Kant adds that the agent has to do what he thinks he ought to do . Therefore our moral judgments have to be separate from our sensibility . The will performs these judgments . Practical reason is acting on the basis of practical freedom . Hume might suggest that for every ` free ' or ` autonomous ' action , and empirical cause can be identified . Kant would probably find on difficulty in defending against this , since our effects are a part of the sensible realm , and these affect other sensible causalities , so that our real autonomous causes are concealed or disguised . We act in uniform and regular ways because we adhere to a universal morality . Both thinkers assume a universal causality . Kant uses this to assert an equally plausible transcendental freedom , whilst Hume thinks causality is a more credible idea because a constant conjunction can be observed . One might object that these grounds are not sufficient for holding it true . Universal causality might not be a necessary feature of scientific pursuits . Compatibilism , of a form more reminiscent of Hume than Kant , is the current widely held view on this ongoing debate . The most recent development however , endorsed by thinkers such as Quine , is that Compatibilism does not preclude determinism , in fact determinism might become redundant in the debate . The reason for this is development in the theory of quantum mechanics , a doctrine that supports the idea that some events are random . By following a quantum mechanics route to freedom we may encounter far more ` outrageous ' suggestions than we could possible point at Kant . Having considered both Hume and Kant 's positions quite thoroughly , we might want to suggest that their theories are not essentially about compatibility at all , but compromise . Both philosophers have to bend , in a sense , the original intuitive problem of freedom . Hume offers us not a freedom without determinism , but instead offers us a freedom that is without constraint . Kant can not assign us causality in a universal , natural sense , but instead construes a causality of freedom , in which he hold all the strings . Despite the popular opinion of what we want form freedom , and the freedom offered to us by each philosopher , the reader might surprisingly feel soothed by Hume 's account , and perturbed by Kant 's .
The word ` sublime ' comes form the Latin ` sublimis ' ; ` sub ' meaning ` up to ' and ` limen ' meaning lintel , the top piece of a door . Its meanings include ` exalted ' or ` raised aloft ' . In aesthetics , the sublime is often a reference to greatness beyond comparison . In more broad contexts this greatness can be artistic , moral or physical . The sublime is something that affects you , something that grips you with awe and terror and challenges the minds usual process of understanding . John Usher exclaimed that the sublime `` takes possession of our attention , and all our faculties , and absorbs them in astonishment . '' The sublime is an experience we are probably all familiar with . We have all been awe-struck by the ferociousness of a storm or the contemplation of the infinity of the universe . One may be struck with the stark contrast between the finite individual and the spatial and temporal infinities of universe . An understanding of the sublime unlocks our most important and exceptional encounters with nature and the arts . F. E. Sparshott says that the `` aesthetic concept -LSB- is -RSB- based on objects that defy conceptualisation . '' This portrayal of the sublime will feature heavily in this essay . This essay will focus on the developments of the theory of the sublime from the late 18 th to the mid-19 th century . We will discuss the transition of the sublime understanding from Burke to Kant in the 18 th century and then consider the work of the Romantic and Mystic Arthur Schopenhauer , writing in the mid-19 th century . These three contemporaries were pivotal figures in the history of the sublime and offered perhaps the most influential and comprehensive accounts of it ever written . Thinkers in the 18 th century recognised a strange ` two-stage ' process in the phenomenon of the sublime experience . The experience that ` takes possession of our attention ' is initially a source of terror , displeasure or threat . However , out of this emerges a feeling of pleasure . The accounts of the sublime in the 18 th - 19 th century attempt to reconcile this apparent paradox . Burke and Kant were the first philosophers to develop the sublime as an essential part of a theory of aesthetics . Burke and Kant are notably similar in the way they view the general differences between the beautiful and the sublime . Both thinkers see the beautiful as light , delightful , appealing and delicate and the sublime as dark , profound , overwhelming and rugged . The sublime not only differs from but is distinct from the beautiful -LRB- they are mutually excusive -RRB- , but their accounts of what the sublime is differ hugely . Both types of experience can produce pleasure in the subject , and of course this is the main paradox that Burke and Kant attempt to address . This is their first major point of departure . Burke 's major work , A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful , was the first work to fully distinguish , with some success , the beautiful from the sublime . Whilst the beautiful produces a direct and benign pleasure , the sublime experience is at once thrilling and exhilarating , an indirectly delightful horror . The sublime object or event , such as the ocean , provides a threat of destruction to the subject . Here there is the sense of danger which creates in the subject a sense of terror or horror , but at the same time the subject is a spectator , shielded from the real threat of destruction . It is an essential part of the sublime experience that the spectator is at a distance or removed from the object or event . The pleasure comes , on the most basic level , from knowing that you are safe in the face of disaster . A more in-depth account of Burke 's process of the sublime experience might be likened to a ` sublime ascent ' . So long as we know the object or event does not really endanger us we can integrate a part of its power and create a likeness to the sublime object . The mind ` rises ' to incorporate a part of the sublime experience . The equality we feel with the sublime object produces a feeling of pleasure and delight within us . We may also feel exalted that we have overcome this threat from nature . One of the strongest feelings we have , as people , is that of self-preservation . The spectator may feel invincible or above the forces of nature . The imagination , as we will later see in Kant , can not provide a conception of infinity or an overwhelming force or the vastness of an ocean , so to compensate it embraces the idea of infinity or force or vastness . The imagination responds to such pressures actively and positively , almost rising to a challenge . Coming into contact with the sublime has a positive effect on us ; it strengthens our powers of conception and , to some extent , our raises our perceived achievements as human beings . Burke 's method of examining the sublime is essentially different from Kant 's . Burke undertakes an empirical exploration of the sublime , and is therefore largely limited to examining the observable effect of the sublime . His position therefore seems based on our psychological and physiological responses to objects and events -LRB- through our senses -RRB- , making his theory a scientific one . Using the example of the ocean we can extrapolate : The entire expanse of water is so large that the eye can not take it all in at once . It stretches the eye beyond its biological capacity , in effect our senses fail to capture the object in the same way that the imagination fails to capture it . In this example there is both a bodily exertion and a cognitive one ; an intelligible and sensible epistemological tension . Likewise eternity and infinity are all sublime because they can not be presented to the mind in a clear and distinct way -LRB- neither the senses nor mind can contain them -RRB- . This shows us the limits of our empirical understanding . The question that seems to remains unanswered after reading Burke is whether ` the sublime ' is the object perceived or the subjective response , or in an interaction of the object and subject . Of course if we commit to saying that what the object provides is a sense of terror , we can not also say it is the direct source of pleasure and this is something that Kant will later pick up on . We might then have to settle on premise that the sublime ` experience ' is the object interacting with the mind , or the imaginations assimilation with the object or spectacle . Burke 's analysis of the sublime is distinctive because he deems that man-made forms of the sublime -LRB- poetry , art -RRB- can be affective . Writers in the 18 th century , such as Milton , create a sense of the sublime in poetry by drawing from a sense of an external , hostile force . Words and language can bring about a renovation of the way one experiences the world . The Sublime is : `` Whatever is in any sort terrible , or is conversant about terrible objects , or operates in a manner analogous to terror '' -LRB- 1.7 P. 36 -RRB- . The same can not be said for visual artists , for although Burke does not suggest that a painting can not evoke a sublime response , but he makes it clear why writing is much more affective . Ignorance , the unknown and confusion are all vital apparatus when it comes to re-creating the sublime . Words alone allow the mind to link incongruent things together and create a more chaotic and overwhelming spectacle . It is difficult to make an idea clear and effecting ; `` To make anything very terrible obscurity in general seems to be necessary . '' -LRB- 2.3 P. 54 -RRB- Burke uses as an example the portrait of Satan in Milton 's Paradise Lost to illustrate the successes of poetical sublime ; `` The images raised by poetry are always of this obscure kind ... '' -LRB- 2.4 P. 57 -RRB- For Burke there need not be a distinction between the sublime experience and its description by talented writers . For Kant , as we shall see , the sublime is a product of raw nature , and can not be re-created in art . Kant is the first to provide an entire philosophical system that incorporated an essential aesthetic theory , which he did in his Critique of Judgment . Kant generally had little interest in the arts , and his philosophical pursuits clearly focus on his love of mathematics , science and logic . He notoriously uses more of a technical vocabulary , of which we will become accustomed to in the following paragraphs . Although Kant shares Burkes aim of tackling the paradox of pleasure produced by something terrible , Kant 's motivation for analysing the sublime is more profound and fundamental for the rest of his philosophical enquires . The sublime proves the existence of the faculty of reason , and reason is necessary for both Kant 's moral project and for the cohesion of Kant 's pure and practical reason . These notions concern Kant 's entire philosophical grounding of which this essay does not afford the prognostics , however we will allude to his general philosophy as and when is necessary and as concisely as possible . Kant 's examples of the natural sublime are quite similar to Burke 's ; the sea , the storm and so on , but his method of investigation is very different . Kant 's assumes a radically transcendental nature of philosophy . This means that when he writes about the sublime he is not suggesting straightforwardly what the sublime is , but what conditions would have to be met in order to have a sublime experience . The essence of the sublime falls wholly on the mind . Kant produces a much more distinctly rationalist approach . The sublime is a subjective condition of perception , but he does not include the psychological terror of Burke . External objects only ` excite ' a process in us . The sublime does not depend on sensation but has an indeterminate relation with concepts . The pleasure arises despite the appearance of the object , not because of it . The sublime can only be attributed to the object through a dislocation in which reason is attached to the formless natural object . It is already evident that , for Kant and Burke , the beautiful and the sublime are mutually exclusive states , however Kant finds some similarities between the sublime and the rest of his aesthetic theory . Both the sublime and the beautiful are disinterested , satisfied and are reflective judgements . This terminology is of little use to this essay , but what is more important to note is that both the sublime and the beautiful demand a universal assent . However , whereas beauty belongs to the judgment of taste , sublimity belongs to the faculty of intellect . The other major differences between the sublime and the beautiful are purposiveness and form . The question of purposiveness , for Kant , is the most significant difference . Beauty seems purposive to its faculty , that is , it seems as if it were fitting or accommodating to our faculty of judgment . The sublime on the other hand is presented as contrapurposive to our reflective faculties , although it is indirectly pleasant and is actually ` felt ' as purposive . Kant thinks that man-made objects -LRB- works of art -RRB- can not be sublime since they would have purpose and lack the contrapurposiveness typical of sublime objects , and the feeling of purposiveness . Sublimity may depend on form , formlessness or deformity , also in the unboundedness of an object and the thought of its totality . Examples of the formlessness are the sea or the storm . Examples of form which exceeds our ability to see it are space and vast mountain ranges . This is unlike the beautiful since the beautiful is bounded by its form . What is judged to be beautiful must be perceived by the imagination as a totality , and this is what the mind contemplates and presents to understanding . The sublime is apprehended by the imagination as unbounded and formless , but reason can conceptualise it as a whole or totality . The formal purposiveness of the beautiful is such that it creates a harmony between the faculties of imagination and understanding . Because of its form , the beautiful object is ` exquisitely cognisable ' . Where the beautiful involves the relation between the imagination and understanding , the sublime involves the relation between imagination and reason . Both of these unions and conflicts produce a subjective purposiveness . The essence of a work of art is its form , and hence this is another reason why works of art -LRB- in Kant 's time -RRB- were unsuccessful at evoking the sublime experience . The process of the sublime experience for Kant fell into two stages . The sublime is initially received as a threat or inhibition of life-enhancing qualities . This state produces in us the effect of displeasure and disturbance . The formlessness of a sublime object is accompanied by a thought of its totality . The mind checks its ability to imagine or represent the object . The imagination plays no positive role , unlike in Burke . It makes a futile attempt to comprehend the incomprehensible before it fails . Despite this failure , but reason can grasp some idea of the totality , thus proving the minds rising above or autonomy from nature , and proving the existence of a reasoning faculty . This effect indirectly gives rise to a feeling of pleasure . That my imagination fails can not prevent me from sustaining an idea of the universe as infinitely great . I can conceive of something my imagination fails to convey . Our faculty of theoretical reason is superior to our faculty of sensibility and imagination . We discover that we have a ` supersensible nature ' , a ` higher purposiveness ' of reason . We become aware of ourselves as rationally autonomous , we become aware of our ` highest vocation ' . This revelation also elevates the human species to an autonomous one that can apply pure and practical reason to moral laws . The sublime for Kant is especially and essentially linked to morality . Kant 's moral theory rests on the idea that human freedom is something outside or above nature ; `` thus found in our own mind a superiority over nature itself even in its immeasurability . '' -LRB- § 28 5:261 P. 145 -RRB- It is our moral freedom that is superior to the omnipotence of nature . This shows we are beings with faculties that transcend the limitations of our finite phenomenal existence . Kant suspected that the indirect pleasure of the sublime experience is largely based on ones cultural and intellectual background , whereas the response to the beautiful object is uncultivated . In fact the degree to which the subject feels the sublime experience is dependent on his dispositions ; `` Feelings of enjoyment or of displeasure rest not so much upon the nature of the external things that arouse them as upon each person 's own disposition to be moved by these pleasures or pains . '' The subject himself must be disposed to receive rational ideas , and for Kant the sublime experience can only occur in a society which already recognises that morality is a consequence of freedom . This means that Kant 's theory relies to some extent on an empirical contingency . This is not an isolated demand , however , since the demand is the same for all theoretical and practical thought , and Kant would say that every human has the transcendental conditions for such recognition . So far we have given a general overview of Kant and have introduced some terminology . One very important aspect of Kant theory that we can not neglect is his distinction between two types of sublime ; the mathematical sublime and the dynamical sublime . In the experience of the Mathematical Sublime the imagination is challenged and defeated by great spatial or temporal magnitude . `` We call sublime that which is absolutely great . '' -LRB- § 25 5:248 P. 131 -RRB- The subjects sense of individuation is lost . The idea of infinity , for example , can not be grasped by the imagination and can be conceived only as an idea of reason . If we tried to conceive of the enormity of the universe , or even the galaxy , the imagination would surely fail because we just can not find any point of comparison . I might start with the size of my own body , and then consider that in relation to the diameter of the earth , and then the earth to the sun , and then the sun to the nearest star and so on . But our attempt failed long ago . The size of my own body just does not compare to the size of the galaxy . In a mathematically sublime encounter , the object or spectacle has to be absolutely great , which is difference from saying that it is merely great . Claims regarding the absolutely great require ` universal assent ' . Even though we compare no numerical values , I would expect others to agree with my views on what is absolutely great . Kant presumes that the judgment is based on a shared , and intuitive , standard of greatness . This measure must be inherent within the object , since there is no external measure of it . The object calls our attention to its own greatness . The mathematical sublime is a rational idea of totality . The Dynamic Sublime is a confrontation with an overbearing power or force , such as storms , volcanoes or hurricanes . The imagination can not act with understanding in such a situation . We find a fear in the overwhelming forces of nature . We become aware of our weaknesses in the face of danger , but this recognition of our deficiency suggests the existence of a higher reflective faculty of reason , which is greater than either nature itself or our imagination . We realise our physical inferiority as natural beings in the face of great natural force , but we become aware of a capacity to judge ourselves as independent of this nature , and this gives us a superiority over nature , after which nature has no domination over us . The dynamic sublime involves an aesthetic estimation of a great natural force or power which has to be resistible and not actually threatening . A real state of fear would render the necessary aesthetic distance impossible . The imagination fails to contain nature as power . We have a ` supersensible ' capacity and vocation that transcends nature . The satisfaction of the sublime does not concern any satisfaction over moral action but shows we are capable of acting according to moral principles . The dynamical sublime is a rational idea of freedom -LRB- from nature and the imagination -RRB- and it demonstrates that the sublime belongs exclusively to human freedom . It is the dynamical sublime as opposed to the mathematical sublime that has a fully developed link to Kant 's moral theory , however he seems to favour the mathematical sublime , or at least indicate that it is the most apparent and clear sublime experience . We might question the extent of the difference between the mathematical and dynamical sublime as Kant himself does not make it clear . For Schopenhauer , this difference is only a matter of degree . Arthur Schopenhauer expounds his theory of the sublime in § 39 of his World as Will and Representation Volume 1 . Although writing 50 years after the Critique of the Power of Judgment , Schopenhauer 's work is somewhat a development of Kant mixed in with Schopenhauer 's fascination with Ancient Indian philosophy that led to his title as a mystic . His theory is very much Post-Kantian in style and he accepts Kant 's philosophy and aesthetics on many levels . Like Kant , Schopenhauer was an Idealist and his theory of the sublime rests upon his Idealism . The vital feature of Schopenhauer 's philosophy and the root of his pessimism is the driving force of mankind ; the will . The will is necessarily negative , something we should strive to overcome , for we are slaves to the will . The will causes all of our suffering and drives us forward to a non-existent end ; `` like the alms thrown to a beggar , which reprieves him today so that his misery may be prolonged until tomorrow '' -LRB- § 38 P. 196 -RRB- . The sublime is integral to his theory of aesthetics because it illustrates an epistemological distinction between knowledge gained from the will and knowledge itself . Art for Schopenhauer was therefore not an indulgence but an intellectual necessity . He saw that in music , the arts and nature we can distance ourselves from the will and attain objectivity . We can contemplate the universal over our individual sufferings . In the beautiful object we perceive the idea of that object , and this suggests a pure knowledge or intelligence that is free from the desires of the will , in effect the will disappears . -LRB- recall what Kant tells us about purposiveness -RRB- . For Schopenhauer the world as will is primary and the world as idea is secondary , and the latter is what we should strive for . In the experience of the beautiful , pure knowledge is attainable without a conflict with the will . The beautiful object itself ensures this because it possesses a certain quality that aids the knowledge of its idea . The will is imperceptibly removed and not even a recollection of it remains . The sublime encounter is also a release from the will , but whereas we derive ` beautiful ' pleasure from a benevolent object that invites our attention , the ` sublime ' pleasure is from encountering a malevolent object . Again , we may imagine the objects of sublime experience to be of the same nature as Burke and Kant conceived them , and Schopenhauer sees these objects as something that threatens the human body and the human will in general , the sublime object ; `` May threaten it by their might that eliminates all resistance , or their immeasurable greatness may reduce it to nought . '' The sublime experience involves a `` violent tearing away from the relations of the same object to the will which are recognized as unfavourable ... '' -LRB- § 39 P. 202 -RRB- . The subject will acknowledge this hostile relation to the will , although he will probably not draw his attention to it , but because this effort is conscious there is always a faint recollection of the will , so the state of exaltation felt by the subject must be consciously maintained . The will that is recollected is not necessarily the individual will but human will in general . This is a typically mystical feature of Schopenhauer , that one sacrifices the individual will for a harmony with the sublime object . Furthermore Schopenhauer points out that if an individual will -LRB- pain or fear etc -RRB- responded to the sublime object -LRB- if the threat or danger were actual -RRB- then this will would fully take control of our consciousness . The idea of peace and contemplation would then be impossible . It is precisely because the will that enters consciousness is willing in general that the subject is able to remove it , and it is because of this that the individual can possess fearlessness in a fearful situation . The initial hostility of the object gives way to a spiritual adulation that arises through the idea of the hostile object . The subject becomes a will-less subject . In this state the subject feels elevated above himself and above his willing . This brings a more profound sense of satisfaction than the beautiful because it involves a struggle and victory over the will . He is left in a euphoric state of contemplation and is filled with this feeling . The subject loses his motivational drive , since this drive is the will itself . One might argue that this state is futile since one could not maintain such a lethargic state of indifference to the world . The subject can not , in this state , strive for happiness since it can not strive for anything . Schopenhauer 's defence would be that the subject is essentially removed from suffering , since there is no will to satisfy or frustrate . Happiness , for Schopenhauer , is also a negative goal , since happiness is nothing more than the cessation of suffering . In terminating the will we are already causing happiness on some level . Kant , as we have previously considered , divided the types of sublime experience into two . Schopenhauer sees the difference between sublime experiences in matter of degree . For Schopenhauer the sublime is distinguished from the beautiful only by levels of addition and the strength of the sublime encounter is a matter of how strong the difference is . The first example of a mild sublime is a winter scene where the sun falls on the ground . We might become aware of the fact that the sun provides light but not warmth . This scene is favourable to knowledge but there is a faint recollection that the lack of warmth is unfavourable to the will and this provides us with a slight challenge to pure knowledge . This is the first transition from beauty to the sublime . The second example is of a lonely region of horizons , such as the North American prairies . Typical of Schopenhauer , his description of this scene should be commended as much for its poetical excellence as its philosophical content . This calls us to serious contemplation and creates a freedom from the will . There is a distinct lack of object and motion ; it is neither favourable nor unfavourable . The will requires there to be a level of strife , but we are left only with pure contemplation . The will is left empty and unoccupied , threatened by the torture and misery of boredom . This example is a perfect challenge ; `` To this extent it affords us a measure of our own intellectual worth , and for this our ability to endure solitude , or our love of it , is a good criterion '' -LRB- P. 204 -RRB- There is an intimate relation between solitude and the sublime . We may have achieved a sublime state of pure knowledge but there is a miserable recollection of the will in need of constant activity . The third example is a region such as the one described above but with no vegetation , only bare rocks . Schopenhauer suggests a desert horizon . The will is shocked and threatened in such a situation by the stark absence of organic objects that sustain life . This creates a certain degree of fear to the will . The fourth example is of nature in turbulent motion , a scene with semi-darkness , black thunder clouds , overhanging cliffs , foaming water or an arid desert . As we struggle with this hostile nature our dependence on the will is broken , as long as we are not personally threatened . The fifth example is of the forces of nature on a large scale ; a roaring waterfall that prevents us fro hearing our own voices , a sea storm , the sight of power beyond all comparison . The twofold nature of our consciousness reaches the highest distinctness . The spectator recognises himself as an individual and a feeble incident of the will . The forces comprehended can destroy him , he is helpless and vanishing into nothingness . At the same time he feels himself as external and a serene object of knowing , the supporter of the whole world . He comes to the realisation that this fearful struggle with nature is only his representation . He is ultimately free from all willing and needs . This bears a notable resemblance to Kant 's Dynamic sublime . The sixth example is of a magnitude in space and time whose immensity reduces the individual to nought . We consider the infinite greatness of the universe , or the infinite time scale of the past or the future . This makes us feel like we reduce to nothing , like drops in an ocean dissolving to nothingness . These feelings arise amidst the conscious recollection that all of these worlds must exist only in our representation . We ourselves are the supporters of these worlds , the vastness of the world rests within us , it depends on us . We merely feel we are one with the world . Schopenhauer felt this linked with the consciousness of the Upanishads of the Vedas ; `` I am all this creation collectively , and besides me there exists no other being . '' -LRB- § 39 P.205-206 -RRB- Many objects can have this effect because of their spatial magnitude or great antiquity . Like Kant , Schopenhauer envisages some dispositional requirement for the sublime experience , perhaps because the tearing-away from the will is a conscious exercise . According to Schopenhauer even the most apathetic person can attain moments of aesthetic pleasure . Plants are the most effective objects of beauty since they have bewildering powers such as a defiance of gravity . Even these , however , do not give us immediate access to knowledge . The observer needs to have a degree of intelligence to bring them into the world of representation and contemplation . Although very different from Kant 's moral theory , Schopenhauer develops a well thought-out theory of sublime character . The ethical sublime denotes a character who is divorced from his will to the point of being master over it , yet still attached sufficiently to provide motivation to live : `` Such a character will ... consider men in a purely objective way , and not according to their relations they might have to his will '' -LRB- § 39 P. 206 -RRB- . In the sublime character the will is not excited by objects that would excite the lay man , and knowledge always attains the upper hand . He can observe men 's faults and happiness without gloat or jealousy . He can perceive the beauty of women without hankering after them . His own happiness or unhappiness will not violently affect him . He looks less at his individual lot than the lot of mankind . He is more of a knower than a sufferer . We have traced the developments and changes of the sublime from the empiricism of Burke , the rationalism and idealism of Kant through to the more esoteric contribution of the idealist , pessimist and mystic Schopenhauer . There are notable similarities between all three thinkers ; they provide similar examples of the sublime in nature and seem to share ideas about what type of object or event can cause a sublime experience . They also all see the solution to the paradox of the ` delightful pleasure ' of the sublime as a two-stage process . Burke examines a terror that the mind assimilates , Kant redeems the failure of the imagination with the success of reason and Schopenhauer overcomes the initial perceived threat to the will with a tearing away from the will leaving a state of pure contemplation . Schopenhauer illustrates the idea that the sublime is an addition to beauty through presenting six gradual stages from the beautiful to the sublime . Burke agrees in places that there are levels of sublime , and that , for example depth of extension is more sublime than length of extension -LRB- 2.7 P. 66 -RRB- , whereas for Kant one need only distinguish between the mathematical and dynamical sublime . This overcomes the danger Kant infringes upon by making the mathematical sublime and the dynamical sublime too distinct and almost separate states . All three to some extent distinguish between the mathematical and the dynamical sublime and prima facie all three seem to regard the mathematical sublime as the most impressive . However for Burke one would presume that power can be portrayed in writing more easily than magnitude , and Kant 's moral theory relies much more heavily on his dynamical sublime . Schopenhauer makes no clear distinction is his writing , suggesting merely that only the last two stages of sublime exemplify the full impression of the sublime . One question that remains unresolved is the question of where the sublime resides . For Burke , being an empiricist , the sublime is the object that fills you with awe and terror , but he does involve some almost psychological processes in his understanding of the sublime , especially when he uses the examples of poetry , suggesting that the role of the mind is quite integral . Kant very adamantly states that the sublime can not be in the object , but to posit that the sublime is not in the object suggests that there is a disposition in the subject but not in the object , i.e. any object could cause a sublime experience , and this is something Kant would not commit to . Schopenhauer calls sublime the object that makes the sublime experience possible , but thereafter focuses on the conscious processes that are imperative for the sublime experience . Overall it would seem that each philosopher might compromise on the position that it is the very interaction between object and subject that is ` sublime ' , it is the two-stage process taken as a whole . With regards to art there is again disagreement . There is indeed a problem in assessing the extent to which the sublime can be re-created in art , and this is just as apparent now as it was for these contemporaries . Burke places perhaps too much emphasis on poetry and his love of language as an evocative medium , sometimes seeming to lose touch with the original idea of the natural sublime . Kant seems to deny art a place in sublime theory ; `` that in art is , after all , always restricted to the conditions of agreement with nature . '' -LRB- sec 23 5:244 P. 129 -RRB- However this passage , as noted in the Guyer edition , at least suggests a possible representation of the sublime in art . Paradoxically Schopenhauer claims that nature aids our transition to a will-less subject more readily than ` plastic ' arts but hailed the arts in general for liberating us from the individual will . He would most likely suggest that the sublime can take place in vague and undefined arts such as music -LRB- as long as they are not pictorial pieces , also note footnote 11 -RRB- . Each philosophical stage we have examined has become more complex , and each philosopher sees the sublime as increasingly important for his whole philosophical grounding . This may provide their own downfall and invite attacks from sceptics who accuse the sometimes vague theory of the sublime as being moulded by the philosophers for their own personal gain . Certainly each philosopher adds a progressively greater success to the human contemplation of the sublime , from Kant 's ` supersensible ' powers to Schopenhauer 's ` sublime character ' . The sublime seems to elevate the human species above all possible expectations , and at the same time we do not lose touch with what is meant by a sublime experience .
For Schopenhauer life is suffering and strife , each man against all others , a Darwinian survival of the fittest , a Hobbesian existence where `` The right of nature ... is the liberty each man hath to use his own power ... for the preservation of his own nature , '' The natural state of man is egoism , we seek our own advantages , and we might imagine men as beetles clambering over each other to get at a food source . This is the picture Schopenhauer paints for us , and although we may not realise it , we all lie and deceive and use whatever means possible in order to get one step ahead of our comrades , as in the man who does a good deed only to receive future homage , or the man who lends money only to secure a future alliance . Schopenhauer , however , noticed that there was one ` feeling ' or ` motivation ' quite unlike all other , and he also noticed that it had been largely ignored or dismissed by other philosophers . This motive was compassion , for Schopenhauer a feeling very real and mysterious , since it makes us want to strive for the good and well-being of another as we normally would do for ourselves , appearing to quite contradict the natural state of man ; `` it is the great mystery of ethics . '' Is compassion therefore unnatural , and whence forth does it stem ? All other motives of action , that is , egoistic ones , are governed by the Will . The Will for Schopenhauer is not an individual will , it is not simply doing ` what one wills ' . The Will is unified and collective , the blind and goalless force behind all of mankinds striving . The grand force of nature and of life , a force that we might liken to our own ideas of evolution or ` mother nature ' . There is no need to question why the Will exists or for what reason it harrows and beleaguers us into action , for this is beyond our realm of knowledge , indeed it has no reason . All that we can examine are its expressions and effects , and that includes our motives of action . If a motive of action is a mere expression of the Will , it is not genuinely moral , since the Will cares not for morality . Schopenhauer was in a sense a precursor to Darwin , anticipating his more scientific theories of evolution and survival instincts . Schopenhauers ' Will provides a unique ` stop gap ' between a religious explanation of the world -LRB- where a deity is the reason and motivation behind all of life -RRB- and a scientific explanation -LRB- where biology and physics give rise to a theory of evolution -RRB- . Indeed his theory is pure metaphysics and will always stand out as something that can not be reduced either to science or religion . That said , his theories provide a great compliment to science . Where Darwin tells us what , Schopenhauer has already told us why , and this is no better portrayed than in his theory of love : We are attracted to strong , healthy individuals , we fall in love , and then we reproduce , hopefully producing strong and healthy children ; this is natural selection . But why the ` love ' bit ? Schopenhauer tells us that we are of such a sentient nature that we realise how abhorred and disgusting the act of reproduction is , and how much suffering life itself causes that if we were of a sane mind no-one would reproduce and the entire species would fail . Therefore the Will provides us with a mechanism that equates love with pleasure and something to be desired . In essence the Will tricks us into thinking that what we most desire in the world is to fall in love and reproduce . Of course when we are married with two screaming infants to deal with we realise that we were mistaken . It is generally believed that as humans we lack instinct and possess free will , but we are in fact very instinctual in matters such as this . I explain this section of Schopenhauers thought to the reader here to clarify what we might expect from the great pessimistic philosopher in accounting for compassion , since I feel that what we might expect is very different from his actual account . In the case of love , the individual is acting for the benefit of the species where he really believes he is acting for himself -LRB- he is pursuing his own pleasure and his own goals -RRB- . The Will has substituted the actual goal for a belief in another . We might view compassion as a motive of action in the same way . The individual needs to strive for his own well-being , since he knows best how to satisfy his desires and fulfill his needs . But a certain degree of looking out for each other might benefit the species as a whole , and it is more than likely that the will would inspire us to do so from time to time . If the whole of humanity were to injure each other and never care for each other , this would be a great detriment to the future of the species . There are cases where we act for the good of another but know deep down that it is benefitting to ourself as in the examples in the introduction , but what could inspire us to help someone poor and dying , who could not possibly repay our actions , or to act heroically to the extent of putting our own life in danger ? The word that Schopenhauer uses to discuss compassion is the German Mitleid , meaning precisely ` to suffer with ' , so essentially we are talking exclusively of suffering . We all suppose that compassion is an act of altruism ; the unselfish concern for the welfare of others , but I wish to propose that we are , in fact , secretly and perhaps unknowingly seeking our own ends . To see another person suffering causes us to suffer , and we act to alleviate their suffering and by doing so alleviate our own . But why should anothers plight cause us to suffer in the first place ? Because the Will dictates it , and why should the Will dictate it ? Because it is tricking us into preserving the species . Compassion seems to be demonstrated at its most instinctual and innate in the case of mother and child , and this is because this is the most integral case of what we generally perceive as altruism . The mother must have a motive for dedicating all of her efforts to the helpless infant without expecting a return on her efforts . But it makes no sense that a man should act for an end that does not benefit him in some way ; such an action would be lacking a motive , would be random . This is where we might suggest that the will provides us with a reason to act , compassion for another causes us to suffer . This account , I believe , fits in perfectly with Schopenhauers account of the Will . Some account of this sort we might expect and perhaps grudgingly accept from someone for whom the will accounts for everything . But we actually find that Schopenhauers views on compassion are of the most contrary and optimistic kind . For him compassion is real and true for the most enlightened souls . On The Basis Of Morality contains Schopenhauers most illusive exposition of compassion . He explains that we can not examine the motives of actions in terms of empirical evidence because we can only observe the action , not the motive behind it ; `` there is always left the possibility that an egoistic motive had influenced the doer of a just or good action . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . However most of us are convinced that `` a man often acts justly , simply and solely that no wrong or injustice may be done to another . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . The people who act in this way are , according to Schopenhauer ` people of true integrity ' . Compassion is therefore a truly altruistic act , and if it were n't then what we call morality would be a futile science without any object , like astrology . Self-interested actions destroy all moral worth of an action because it is an act of survival only , but compassion is excluded from self-interested motives ; `` The absence of all egoistic motivation is , therefore , the criterion of an action of moral worth . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . All of our motivations are caused by our ` weal and woe ' , but compassion causes us to focus solely on the weal and woe of another . How does it cause us to do this ? `` If my action is to be done simply and solely for the sake of another ; then his weal and woe must be directly my motive . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- ; in other words the other person becomes the object of my will ; `` I suffer directly with him . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . I do not mistake his suffering for my own , I feel his suffering in me . I have suggested that an evolutional account involving the Will could explain that although a mans compassion is real , it is not really altruism , since acting other than for yourself is no motive to act at all ; we are always acting self-interestedly , but not alway egoistically . But our predictions for a Schopenhauerian theory of compassion were very wrong , and here Schopenhauer lives up to his name as a mystic and appeals to Eastern thought . Morality can only be examined metaphysically , and compassion is a boundary mark that only metaphysics can overstep . The compassionate person has true metaphysical insight because they can identify with someone elses suffering as if it were their own . They are thus looking beyond the individual to the complete unification . They recognise not only their own individual suffering , but the suffering of mankind , indeed of all life , and they realise that everything expresses the will to life , which `` extends our interest to all that lives , '' -LRB- WWR .66 -RRB- . This new insight into the world shows us that all is one ; we have pierced the veil of Maya and seen beyond the world of representation where individuation acts as a partition . He hails his own ` supreme principle ' of ethics ; `` Injure no-one ; on the contrary , help everyone as much a you can '' -LRB- BM .16 . fn20 -RRB- and sees compassion as the highest cardinal virtue , and a virtue of action at that . This is certainly quite a powerful Buddhist insight and one that influenced many aspects of his thought . One can not help but wonder , however , whether this was just an aspect of his fancy . There are several reasons why Schopenahauers compassion seems at best a slight inconsistency in his theory : -LRB- 1 -RRB- Compassion is not and can not be a product of reason , it is a non-cognitive feeling . It is an intuition that we can not express rationally ; `` It is a direct and intuitive knowledge that can not be reasoned away or arrived at by reasoning , '' -LRB- WWR .66 -RRB- . Unlike Kant , Schopenhauer thinks you can not derive morality from a theory of abstract reasoning , and compassion is not merely a feeling but a human essence ; a superhuman quality . But the will itself instills in us our intuitions and so would surely be the producer of feelings of compassion . At the same time compassion conflicts with the wills interests of self-preservation . Schopenhauer seems to be suggesting something he can not be ; that the will has provided us with a way of overriding it . -LRB- 2 -RRB- Virtue can not be taught , people can only gain knowledge from compassion , but our characters are unalterable . However much we praise compassion , an individual either is or is not compassionate . If it were actually an expression of Will , however , everyone would have the capacity for compassion . Indeed we might say that we observe women to be more compassionate than men . Women are by nature the nurturing sex and so it would be reasonable for them to be more instinctively compassionate . I feel Schopenhauer would have difficulty admitting here that it is because women are more knowing and enlightened . -LRB- 3 -RRB- Compassion is sometimes felt , but is not always a motive of action . We might feel sympathy for or pity the beggar on the streets , the plight of the blue cross on an advert might cause us to express an ` awww ' , but we do not act . Schopenhauer recognises the difference between feeling and acting by illustrating that the just person feels compassion , and the noble person acts on it . Why should it serve as a motive for some and not others ? It would seem undeniable that there is a special quality to compassion . Whether or not it is governed by the will , or allows us to see beyond the Will , it is the only action in which the agent truly identifies with the subject and believes himself to be acting altruistically . If we are to define a genuine moral motive of action as that which is not egoistic , then I agree with Schopenhauer that compassion is eligible . For Schopenhauer , however , altruism is real , although I believe it would be more compatible with his theory of the will to hold that the Will is the force behind this apparent altruism , since an action of absolutely no benefit to oneself -LRB- either direct or indirect , conscious or unconscious -RRB- seems absurd or at least ambitious . Whatever the answer , compassion breaks away from egoism and is therefore worthy of the title of a ` genuine moral motive of action ' , just as those who are in love are really and truly in love , whatever the cause of this may be . Compassion is surely the point of unusual optimism for Schopenhauer , a twist we never expected . It is truly an enlightening theory from which the great pessimist gives us the greatest surprise .
The moral philosophy of Schopenhauer and Nietzsche could not , it seems , be more opposing ; Schopenhauer glorifies the moral significance of life whilst life for Nietzsche has no intrinsic moral meaning , Schopenhauer 's greatest aspiration is to deny the will whilst Nietzsche only affirms it , for Schopenhauer suffering devalues life and yet for Nietzsche suffering inspires creativity , strength and self-overcoming . This idea of suffering , and its counterpart ; compassion , form a pivotal point of departure between these two writers . Compassion , for Nietzsche , was an important topic because `` the problem of the value of pity conjures up doubt , mistrust , fear , and the belief in all morality wavers . '' -LRB- GMp6 -RRB- , however it was , for Schopenhauer , the basis of morality ; a basis which Nietzsche viewed as grossly misguided . This essay will form a study on the merits and demerits of Nietzsche 's criticisms of compassion and subsequently his criticisms of Schopenhauer . It would conform to tradition to clarify here what I intend by the word compassion , as used in the title . Although perhaps not strictly necessary and a little tedious , we are examining , in English , a German word whose meaning may have warped through translation , and so this discussion should satisfy the discerning reader . Both Nietzsche and Schopenhauer use the word ` mitleid ' , and in Schopenhauer it is accepted that what he means is a direct translation to ` compassion ' , or more accurately ` suffering with ' . Readers of Nietzsche have disagreed as to whether Nietzsche is using Mitleid in the sense of compassion or pity . The difference is thought to be significant because whilst compassion is commonly heralded as benevolent and selfless , pity can sometimes also be interpreted as ` looking down upon ' . If Nietzsche is condemning pity in this sense his position is more understandable and less startling than if he were condemning pure compassion , and it will become obvious that Nietzsche see 's mitleid as a way of possibly gaining power over the sufferer . The German word ` Mitleid ' , however , translates equally into English as both compassion and pity . The English word ` compassion ' , according to the Oxford Dictionary of English , means ` sympathetic pity and concern for the sufferings or misfortunes of others ' , and pity is ` feelings of sorrow and compassion caused by sufferings or misfortunes of others ' . General semantic acceptance then seems to view these words as pretty much interchangeable ; ` pity ' as a ` looking down upon ' seems to have entered general meaning through misuse or misinterpretation . Regardless of these definitions , what is clear is that whatever Nietzsche is addressing , he -LRB- rightly or wrongly -RRB- takes to be the same condition as the one Schopenhauer is addressing , and his discussions of mitleid are almost direct responses to him . This essay will therefore use the terms pity and compassion interchangeably and the quotes I use will remain faithful to the particular translations I am quoting from . On The Basis Of Morality contains Schopenhauer 's most illusive exposition of compassion . He explains that we can not examine the motives of actions in terms of empirical evidence because we can only observe the action , not the motive behind it ; `` there is always left the possibility that an egoistic motive had influenced the doer of a just or good action . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . However most of us are convinced that `` a man often acts justly , simply and solely that no wrong or injustice may be done to another . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . The people who act in this way are , according to Schopenhauer ` people of true integrity ' . Compassion is therefore a truly altruistic act , and if it were n't then what we call morality would be a futile science without any object , like astrology . Self-interested actions destroy all moral worth of an action because it is an act of survival only , but compassion is excluded from self-interested motives ; `` The absence of all egoistic motivation is , therefore , the criterion of an action of moral worth . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . All of our motivations are caused by our ` weal and woe ' , but compassion causes us to focus solely on the weal and woe of another . How does it cause us to do this ? `` If my action is to be done simply and solely for the sake of another ; then his weal and woe must be directly my motive . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- ; in other words the other person becomes the object of my will ; `` I suffer directly with him . '' -LRB- BM .16 -RRB- . I do not mistake his suffering for my own , I feel his suffering in me . Morality can only be examined metaphysically , and compassion is a boundary mark that only metaphysics can overstep . Nietzsche 's views on morality , and especially compassion could not be further from Schopenhauer 's . He agrees with Schopenhauer that the only true moral action is an altruistic one , and if compassion were a truly altruistic moral action it may have saved moral value from contempt , however he asserts that egoism is behind mitleid , as with all of our actions , not altruism : `` Benefiting and hurting others are ways of exercising one 's power upon others . '' -LRB- GS13 -RRB- Nietzsche therefore endorses moral scepticism , denying Schopenhauer 's meta-ethical realism , for all morality rests on egoistic grounds . For him morality is a ` futile science ' . Nietzsche also criticises Schopenhauer 's lack of evidence . Schopenhauer uses metaphysics to explain the moral significance of actions but Nietzsche suggests that his metaphysics are dictated by his commitment to save morality and not visa versa . In Beyond Good and Evil -LRB- 186 -RRB- Nietzsche remarks that ; `` Philosophers one and all ... wanted to furnish the rational ground of morality '' and that `` Schopenhauer too failed to do it '' . In the Anti-Christ also , Nietzsche claims ; `` Schopenhauer was hostile to life : therefore pity became for him a virtue '' . Nietzsche thinks that Schopenhauer is misconstruing compassion , he lacks a psychological understanding of it because he lacks experience of it ; `` those who glorify pity lack experience of it in the realm of morality '' -LRB- D133 -RRB- . He is adamant that '' ` There is no moral yardstick '' -LRB- D139 -RRB- and that Schopenhauer is being led by his heart and not his reason . In the Gay Science -LRB- 99 -RRB- , Nietzsche accuses Schopenhauer of `` mystical embarrassments and subterfuges '' due to his fascination with Eastern philosophies , and in the same aphorism claims that Schopenhauer 's theory is `` nonsense about pity , about how it breaks through the principle of individuation and how it is the source of all morality '' . For Nietzsche compassion is remarkable , but not because it is a ` boundary mark of metaphysics ' ; compassion should be discouraged since it only brings forth more suffering in the world , a central argument that will be supported later in the essay . Nietzsche 's writing forms the antithesis of Schopenhauer 's compassion . In Daybreak -LRB- esp . 133 -RRB- Nietzsche 's explanation of why we might leap into the water after someone is far from altruistic . The thoughtless person , he says , puts it down to pity . Nietzsche elucidates that granted , few people would admit to consciously thinking of themselves at such a time , however we have a very strong unconscious egoistic motivation . He goes so far as to say that another 's hardship offends us to the point that we would feel shame and cowardice if we did not help . Our reaction to this offence is to feel compassion out of self-defence or even revenge . Power and overcoming others is a significant feature in Nietzsche , and we play the part of the more powerful helper because we expect applause and recognition , and we choose to play an active part in dramas of suffering perhaps to alleviate our boredom . Pure pleasure can also arise in such situations when we contrast the sufferer 's position to our own . The thought that we can help if only we choose to and the expectance of praise and homage also make pity pleasurable and therefore pity has many constituents . `` Even if we offer ourselves as martyrs do for their church this is a sacrifice that is offered for our desire for power , '' -LRB- GS13 -RRB- therefore an action from compassion never lacks a reason , and this reason is much less mystical than Schopenhauer 's unification principle . One criticism of Schopenhauer 's theory is that the compassionate person seems to lack agency , acting always for the will . It may seem more realistic and even optimistic to hold that the person acts compassionately because they are acting autonomously and with agency , and because it will benefit them in some way . Pity , for Nietzsche , is to be condemned because it is a weakness , and causes more suffering in the world : `` suffering itself becomes contagious through pity '' -LRB- A7 -RRB- . This is partly explained in the same aphorism , as Nietzsche puts it ; `` Pity stands in antithesis to the tonic emotions which enhance the energy of the feeling of life : it has a depressive effect '' and `` The loss of force which life has already sustained through suffering is increased and multiplied even further by pity '' . This is in direct contrast to Schopenhauer , who , `` has made it -LSB- pity -RSB- the virtue '' -LRB- A7 -RRB- and for whom no action could better demonstrate moral value . What Schopenhauer fails to acknowledge , however , and what Nietzsche see 's as an important point is the role of the sufferer in conjunction with that of the compassionate person . He recognizes that there is something degrading in suffering and elevating in pitying . When we pity we are being unintentionally cruel ; `` when I helped him , then I sorely injured his pride '' -LRB- D134 -RRB- . In the Gay Science -LRB- 338 -RRB- Nietzsche notes that the sufferer generally hides his suffering , since suffering is a sign of weakness ; `` All arousing of pity and calling for help is seductive , for our ` own way ' is too hard . '' If others notice our suffering they tend to assume access to the deepest recesses of our heart , but in fact they evaluate us superficially . Pity therefore takes away what is personal to the sufferer . The kindest way to be compassionate , if we must be compassionate , is from a distance ; `` I do n't want to be recognized for being compassionate '' -LRB- Z ` Of the Compassionate -RRB- he tells us , for the compassionate are too lacking in shame and the sufferer would be shamed by our compassion . This is in no way unkindness on Nietzsche 's part ; on the contrary it is a warning , a recommendation for a better world . Far from the ` boot-in-the-face ' picture of Nietzsche presented by some , in the Gay Science -LRB- 338 -RRB- he urges us ; `` to share not suffering but joy '' . Our time would be better spent leaning how to enjoy ourselves rather than learning how to be compassionate since it is better that we unlearn how to harm others . This draws out another detail not found Schopenhauer 's theory ; a differentiation between types of compassion , such as compassion for friends and compassion for strangers . Since Schopenhauer 's principle of individuation renders all individuals identical in their suffering we are supposed to treat all men equally . That , in reality , is not the case . `` Can you go near to your friend without going over him ? '' Nietzsche asks in Thus Spoke Zarathustra -LRB- ` Of Friends ' -RRB- . Considering how humiliating compassion can be , it is hardly something we should endeavor to inflict upon our friends : `` let your pity for your friend conceal itself under a hard shell '' he advises us . It is all too easy for us to wish to relieve the pain of a friend , whilst underneath our supposed ` suffering with ' him , we are really relishing their active gratitude and homage , indeed one may be familiar with the feeling of rejection often felt when a friend in need has refused our help , when really we should be admiring his independence and strength . `` If you have a suffering friend , be a resting place for his suffering , but a resting place like a hard bed , a camp bed ; thus you will serve him best . We can therefore , if we are careful , do our friends an honour by providing for him with the right intentions , however ; `` strangers and the poor must pluck from my tree for themselves - it causes less shame . '' -LRB- Z ` Of the Compassionate ' -RRB- Here , it seems , Nietzsche is far from the selfish egoist he is often supposed to be . Schopenhauer too assumes there is something beyond compassion . His theory of the Saint , the highest human being , see 's through the futility of recognising a shared suffering and see 's compassion itself as futile . Compassion will not free us from the Will , only a life of self-directed suffering will allow us to deny it . Nietzsche has strong views on Christianity in general , and this ties in with compassion because many moral doctrines , such as ` love thy neighbour ' are produced by Christianity ; `` nothing in our unhealthy modernity is more unhealthy than Christian pity '' -LRB- A7 -RRB- . In Daybreak -LRB- 132 -RRB- he claims that Schopenhauer and his theories of pity and sympathy merely echo Christianity . `` Christianity is called the religion of pity , '' -LRB- GS338 -RRB- and Nietzsche suggests that Christians praise pity only because it allows them to have power and superiority over others . In perhaps his most famous quote , Nietzsche makes the claim that `` God is dead ; '' -LRB- Z ` Of the Compassionate ' -RRB- Fragmented like this it seems he is merely an atheist , but the full quote reads ; `` God is dead ; god has died of his love for man . '' Nietzsche is concerned with the destructive role of compassion , and perhaps uses god to attack Christian pity . Schopenhauer , although also an atheist , praises Christianity and Buddhism for their morality , Nietzsche attacks them on the same grounds . For Schopenhauer life is suffering . For Nietzsche it is a struggle for survival . Both have a Darwinist flavour in places , but where Schopenhauer departs onto mystical grounds , Nietzsche remains more rooted ; `` The enlightened man goes among men as among animals . '' -LRB- Z ` Of the Compassionate ' -RRB- The natural way is the best way , we should follow our will . In an echo of the ` survival of the fittest ' , compassion destroys evolution because it `` preserves what is ripe for destruction '' -LRB- A7 -RRB- , preserving everything that is miserable and allowing the weak to fester . Brutal and starkly direct , Nietzsche has been branded a pessimist , nihilist , fascist and has been generally misunderstood for decades , and yet , despite his notoriety , he had become perhaps the most widely read and analysed philosopher . In many ways Nietzsche deserves to be defended against claims that his philosophy is heavy-handed and dangerous , lacking the delicacies offered by Schopenhauer 's compassion . Schopenhauer 's ` supreme principle ' of ethics ; `` Injure no-one ; on the contrary , help everyone as much as you can '' , is not a far cry from Nietzsche 's `` share not suffering but joy '' , indeed they could have been written by the same hand . His attacks on compassion are also , as we have clarified , and attack on revenge and cruelty . As Nussbaum points out ; `` A true self-creator does not need power over others . '' Nietzsche not only criticises but develops Schopenhauer 's thinking about compassion -LRB- and would no doubt feel indebted to him for highlighting the importance of the issue -RRB- , considering the role and position of the sufferer in relation to the compassionate , the difference between the suffering friend and the suffering stranger , and even the Darwinian evolutional role . In some ways Nietzsche appears more pessimistic than Schopenhauer , focusing on egoism , the animalistic nature of humans , strength and power , and yet at the same time his words are more empowering , more elevating and perhaps more honest .

Hundreds of years ago , Saint Augustine explained language acquisition by stating that babies learn by listening to the words parents use when pointing to objects around them -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- . Whilst this account might seem intuitive , and does play some role in an infant 's learning -LRB- Harris , 1983 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- , it is far from explaining the complicated language acquisition process in its entirety . Gopnik et al -LRB- 2003 -RRB- point out that children do not only learn names of objects , but verbs , adjectives and prepositions too . They have to distinguish human speech from other sounds in their environment , break down this continuous sound-stream into separate , meaningful words and understand different voices and pronunciations . Whilst these are hurdles which the most modern computers are nowhere near overcoming , normally developing children are able to conquer understanding and producing grammatical language to an almost adult-like standard by the time they are four years old -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- . This essay will explain how the preferences infants seem to possess innately help them to use the abilities they all have to help them to master their native language in such a short time . Our ability to perceive and attend to language begins even before we are born . A foetus ' inner ears are fully grown at 20 weeks from conception -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- and soon are working well enough to hear the sounds conducted through the mother 's abdomen and the amniotic fluid . We know this because unborn babies do not only move in response to loud noises but because they have also become tuned into their mother 's voice , and learned to distinguish it from other women 's , by the time they are born -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- . Furthermore , when only a day old , infants can not only already distinguish human voices from other noise in their environment , but preferential sucking tasks show they prefer to listen to voices over anything else -LRB- Decasper & Fifer , 1980 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- . This suggests that babies are already programmed to be attentive to language by the time they are born . What is even more fascinating is that newborns have the ability to remember words , as well as the sounds of voices , that they have heard prenatally . Snow -LRB- 1977 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- showed this by giving mothers in their last three months of pregnancy a story to read aloud frequently . As expected , one day-old infants chose to hear a recording of their mother -LRB- whose voice they were familiar with -RRB- reading this story than a stranger . However , given the choice , they preferred to listen to a stranger 's voice reading the familiar story than their mothers reading a different one . This highlights that foetuses can in some way attend to the content of the language they hear as well as how the voice speaking it sounds . Therefore it seems babies are born with a preference to listen and tune into voices and words they have heard , remembered and become familiar with . This early preference to focus attention on speech sounds will be vital for helping the babies to learn about language and later produce it themselves . As well as being able to tune into and remember the language they hear , babies seem to be more sensitive to some speech sounds than adults . This is especially apparent when regarding the differences between phonemes . In English , the sounds / r / and / l / are pronounced , heard and spelt differently . So if a recording plays sounds where / r / gradually becomes / l / , English adults will perceive a sudden change from one consonant to the other -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- : we turn a graded input into distinct categories . Conversely , Japanese adults , who do not distinguish between these phonemes in their language , will perceive no sudden change -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- . Whilst adults seem constrained by their mother tongues , babies under six months old who have only been exposed to one of English , Hindi and Salish can discriminate the phonemes of the other languages as well as their own -LRB- Butterworth et al , 2002 -RRB- . This initial high sensitivity to many changes in language sounds means humans have the ability and potential to learn any language they are exposed to , regardless of their race or genetic heritage . However , this impressive ability to distinguish so many phonemes soon diminishes . The Japanese babies will no longer perceive / r / and / l / differently after they are around six months old -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- when their phoneme recognition ability becomes similar to an adult 's : ` exposure to a particular language has altered their brains ' -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 , p. 104 -RRB- . Gopnik et al -LRB- 2003 -RRB- believe this process is caused by babies listening to what we say and constructing prototypes for each phoneme ; ` grouping the sounds they hear into the right categories , the categories their particular language uses ' -LRB- p. 108 -RRB- so English babies will develop separate / r / and / l / prototypes , but the Japanese will include both sounds in one . Whilst such narrowing may seem restrictive to a baby 's potential , in fact this eventual ` learning by forgetting ' -LRB- Duplox & Mehler , 1994 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- means that they will be able to focus more attention on understanding the phoneme distinctions that matter in their own language . This will help them later to recognise and learn whole words and their meanings . As well having equal potential to learn any language and preferring to listen to human voices over other sounds , four day-old babies have already learnt to recognise their own language . French babies of this age prefer listening to French over Russian -LRB- Butterworth and Harris , 2002 -RRB- . They seem to be able to make this distinction by paying close attention to various sound features ; as well as phoneme categories babies recognise the prosodic and rhythmic features of the language they hear . For example , a two month old English baby will distinguish more between Japanese and English recordings than Dutch and English recordings , presumably because Dutch has similar stress patterns to English -LRB- Christophe & Morton , 1998 -RRB- and they will prefer to listen to languages with similar rhythms to English -LRB- Cutler et al , 1993 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- . This awareness of the many distinguishing features language sounds have and the preference they have for listening to their native language sounds will also help infants to concentrate on and begin to understand more aspects of how their language works . Despite the fact that newborns will not begin to produce these speech-sounds they which are so interested in for a few months -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- , they show an ability to initiate and maintain some kind communication with adults from much earlier on . Most obviously , from birth they have the ability to cry to communicate distress and in the first few weeks some parents claim they can distinguish a ` hungry ' from a ` tired ' cry -LRB- Evesham , 1989 -RRB- . From six weeks , babies smile in response to their mothers ' voices -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- which will presumable encourage her to speak more to them . This will be helpful because the more a baby is spoken to , the faster its language learning will progress ; unlike other knowledge , there is no way of finding out about language other than listening to those who use it -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- . Babies show delight in games that involve turn-taking , such as peekaboo , highlighting the social context in which early language development begins -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- that babies seem to be keen to participate in ; for Kugimutzakiz -LRB- 1999 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- their ` untimate motive is a deep seated need to communicate ' -LRB- p. 129 -RRB- . Making adults see them as social creatures ensures babies are exposed to language which they will be able to listen to and learn from . Of course , for babies to be eventually able to produce language themselves , they will have to be able to reproduce these words they hear . Long before language-like sounds are produced , babies show keenness to imitate adults . Meltzoff & Moore -LRB- 1977 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- found that newborns will not only copy an experimenter who sticks out his tongue , but will make an obvious effort to find their own tongues -LRB- Meltsoff & Moore , 1994 , 1997,1999 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- . This suggests that babies already regard imitation as a worthwhile pursuit . Furthermore , it is interesting that the tests of imitation with the most significant results involve the organs of articulation , since it is essential that babies master motor skills in this area for them to be able to produce the hundreds of different sounds in their future language -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- . Once babies are in the ` babbling ' stage , this drive to imitate is applied to language sounds too . Between two and five months of age , the percentage of infant sounds matched to what the mother had just produced increases from 27 % to 43 % , with pitch being the most frequently replicated feature , followed by duration and rhythm -LRB- Papousek & Papousek , 1989 -RRB- . Gopnik et al -LRB- 2003 -RRB- tell us that when five month-olds heard an / ee / vowel sound for 15 minutes , they responded with a similar cooing ; ` just hearing a grown-up produce the sound motivates babies to try and produce it themselves ' -LRB- p. 125 -RRB- . Babies also pay close attention to the visual display played alongside the sound -LRB- Kugimutzakiz , 1999 , in Butterworth & Harris , 2002 -RRB- perhaps to work out how it is made , before testing this out by trying to produce the sound themselves . Such behaviour demonstrates babies ' strong desire to practice the sounds they have learned to make . Butterworth & Harris -LRB- 2002 -RRB- identify a stage where this is most obvious , called ` vocal play ' . They tell us it emerges after a change in neuronal networks at 3-9 months of age , which renders the baby better able to coordinate motor movements with the vocal organs . Infants ' increasing expertise allows them to experiment with different volumes , pitches and tongue positions familiarising them with ' a kind of mouth-to-sound gap ' -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- which they seem to delight in discovering . Such desires to practice using their mouths to produce different kinds of sounds will be a useful route for the babies to perfect their pronunciation of the phonemes making up their future language . The abilities and preferences of infants discussed so far may explain how infants learn individual words as labels , but they do not allow for the thousands of grammatical rules children learn with regard to how to put these words together into sentences and how to give these sentences meaning . I will now explain how grammar would be impossible to learn through imitation alone . The most obvious example to consider is that children in all cultures often make mistakes by over-regularising irregular word forms . -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- . They might use the past tense suffix ' - ed ' to produce words like ` goed ' or ` eated ' or the suffix 's ' to indicate plurality for words where this is not normally used , for example ` sheeps ' or ` foots ' . It is unlikely that the babies would have heard these words from adults , so they can not be produced through imitation alone . These mistakes can not be accidental ; four year olds introduced to a made-up word such as ` wug ' will infer the plural form is ` wugs ' -LRB- Gopnik et al , 2003 -RRB- . To produce the above mistakes illustrate that infants have grasped the concept that words are divided into morphemes and affixes can alter their meanings . To use these affixes appropriately , they must also understand the concepts of plurality and tense . Therefore these mistakes , although non-standard English , are actually intelligent because they illustrate the infant has the ability to apply grammatical rules generally and to novel words , rather than by learning each instance separately . In conclusion , babies seem to be designed to acquire language even before they are born . They have the abilities to tune into human voices , remember familiar words and sounds and encourage adults to communicate and teach them . Furthermore , they seem to desire and actively seek exposure to language and choose to practice what they learn until it is perfected . Once given the cue of exposure to adult speech , and time for some practicing , they are able to grasp the rules of grammar and begin to make their native language their own .
Archer -LRB- 2001 -RRB- claims the reason we ` think as we do today ' is ` natural selection has made us this way ' -LRB- p. 414 -RRB- . Reviewing the literature , it seems Evolutionary Psychologists call on Darwinian Theory of biological evolution - that the genes for physical properties which aid human survival will be passed on more than those that do not , because individuals possessing those genes are more likely to survive and reproduce - and apply this to psychological dispositions to think and behave today in terms of survival . This essay will discuss claims made by evolutionary psychologists , as well as various criticisms of their methods . I will focus mainly on the issues of attraction , mate selection methods and criteria and rape , because these are areas causing controversy amongst academics . I have interpreted the title quotation as follows : by ` single unifying starting point ' Archer implies we should begin all psychological enquiry by first asking what help the given behaviour would have been to our ancestors in surviving to pass on their genes ; by saying ` why we think as we do today ' I assume Archer agrees with my view that thoughts are expressed in behaviour , so I will consider thoughts in this context to be the conscious or unconscious motivation behind actions . I will discuss the strength of his and other evolutionary psychologists ' claims with specific examples in this essay . In my opinion , the greatest appeal of Evolutionary psychology is that is ` makes sense ' . Issues such as why we are more prone to irrational phobias of spiders -LRB- Campbell , 2001 -RRB- than cars , which are more lethal to us , can be explained simply and logically with ideas like this : the early humans who were scared of spiders avoided them , so were not killed by the poisonous ones and survived to reproduce , thus we have inherited the psychological tendency to avoid them . Cars are a recent invention and evolution has not yet caught up with industry ; ` the lag time between adaptive problems and solutions ' -LRB- Campbell , 2001 , p. 426 -RRB- . Archer -LRB- 2001 -RRB- claims another benefit ; that Evolutionary Psychology uncovers knowledge which would not be examined if generating hypotheses from ` conventional ' -LRB- p. 414 -RRB- methods . He says that ` by viewing present-day mechanisms as reflections of past adaptations ' -LRB- 2001 , p. 414 -RRB- and theorising accordingly , discoveries have been made which would not have been found using ` culturally based explanations of social behaviour ' -LRB- p. 430 -RRB- . I would say that the main objections to Evolutionary Psychology are that first , explanations such as the above phobia theory seem too simplified . Secondly , the theories are hard to prove because they are often based on ` indirect evidence , notably broad evolutionary principles , cross-species comparisons , cross-cultural surveys and present day human behaviour ' -LRB- Archer , 2001 -RRB- and so the claims made are wide open to criticisms . Thirdly , these ` explanations ' have been considered socially irresponsible -LRB- e.g. Rose & Rose , 2001 -RRB- because of the worry that immoral behaviour might be excused by a claim of biological pre-disposition . As Archer -LRB- 2001 -RRB- says , ` science is located in the social world ; it is subject to social pressures derived from political and social beliefs ' -LRB- p. 430 -RRB- . These criticisms will be discussed throughout the essay by critically examining various Evolutionary Psychology theories . First I will discuss attraction . Fredrickson et al -LRB- 2003 -RRB- tell us although people do not rank physical attractiveness as important in how much we like someone , various studies have shown it is . However , this research has mainly occurred under test conditions with unknown people . Obviously with first impressions , looks are all we have to judge someone by . In real relationships , other factors cause us to like other people . These include the convenience of being close-by , people being familiar to us and similarity ; people tend to marry people of the same nationality , religion , class and educational status as themselves -LRB- Fredrickson et al. , 2003 -RRB- . These factors do not seem to be caused by evolution , but more by culture and convenience ; for example most religions encourage same-faith partnerships , and if you meet your spouse at University you will obviously have similar educational backgrounds -LRB- Fredrickson et al. , 2003 -RRB- . To expand on this topic , Evolutionary Psychology expects the sexes to select partners differently due to their different levels of input in reproduction . Since the male role in mating need only take minutes -LRB- or seconds -RRB- and supplies of sperm are limitless -LRB- men have no monthly cycles or menopause -RRB- they can potentially father hundreds of children . However , a female spends nine months pregnant and more nursing ; she can have far less children and her time investment in each child is greater -LRB- Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- . These observations have been used by evolutionary psychologists to explain the findings that women are ` choosier ' than men when approached for either a date or sex in a bar -LRB- Buss , in Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- and why there are more societies allowing male polygamy than female -LRB- Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- . According to evolutionary theory , men should take any available opportunity to spread their genes , but as a women can only have limited offspring she should ensure she only mates with good quality men to ensure the best genes and increase her offspring 's chances of survival . However , this theory does have some problems . If a man impregnates hundreds of women but does not look after his offspring they will die before they can reproduce , and his genes will not be passed on . This could explain how today 's tendency for monogamous relationships has ` evolved ' . As Fredrickson et al -LRB- 2003 -RRB- point out , Evolutionary Psychology could be used to explain both male infidelity and faithfulness . Does this make sense ? Some critics might take the view that evolutionary psychologists could invent any story about early humans and make it fit to how they want to view the world today . Fredrickson et al -LRB- 2003 -RRB- mention studies by Peele and Hatfield -LRB- 1988 ; 1988 -RRB- which found that long-lasting relationships begin with what they call ` passionate love ' , with intense sexual attraction , but as the relationships progress passion subsides and gives way to ` companionate love ' which is a strong intimate and trusting bond . The tendency for relationships to so progress could have evolved so couples stay together to rear young , although not all relationships progress like this , some do break down . It seems to me an equally reasonable explanation that people grow to love each other because of the reasons illustrated above ; they are familiar and similar to each other and these social factors might be what determines whether their bonds last . The development of companionate love could also explain why so many arranged marriages do work out - Gupta and Singh -LRB- 1982 , cited in Hogg and Vaughan , 2002 -RRB- found that although Indian arranged marriage couples initially rated lower levels of love for one another than ` love matches , ' over time this reversed . A Western equivalent is the rising popularity of dating agencies ; we are placing trust in more qualified others to find a long-term partner compatible with us rather than trusting our own attraction instincts -LRB- Hogg & Vaughan , 2002 -RRB- . Another area of relationships Evolutionary Psychology attempts to explain is the differences in criteria the sexes use to select partners . For example Buss -LRB- 2003 , in Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- points out women are far likelier than men to consider economic wealth and this is a strong predictor for the effort she will give to maintaining the relationship -LRB- Buss , 2001 -RRB- . Men tend to place more emphasis on physical attractiveness and youth . Evolutionary psychologists could theorise that this female choice has evolved because she needs a provider while she is busy with nursing to help her offspring survive . However , as Buss and Barnes -LRB- 1986 , cited in Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- suggest , this criteria is not necessarily evolved ; it could be because of practical and social reasons . Until relatively recently , with the development of contraception , women spent most of their adult lives pregnant and breastfeeding . This means men have got jobs , got involved in politics and gained the positions of power , ` excluding women from any other opportunity to obtain economic wealth ' -LRB- Buss & Barnes , 1986 , in Fredrickson et al , 2003 , p. 672 -RRB- than through men . This idea is similar to social exchange theory -LRB- Hogg & Vaughan , 2002 -RRB- which applies economic concepts to relationships ; we put in the resources we expect to get out of them , whether in the form of money , love , time , services or social status . We will give what is available to us , and seek what we do not have , which makes practical sense without appeal to natural selection . However , Buss -LRB- 2001 -RRB- argues that since his information comes from 37 countries -LRB- a ` cross-cultural ' survey method -RRB- including Scandinavian countries where the sexes are approaching equal incomes , the fact that these criteria still emerge means women must have some sort of genetic tendency to obtain resources . However , I would contest this claim as the women 's movement only occurred within the last couple of generations . It is likely that cultural patterns and social norms such as what women should look for in a man will remain for quite a while , especially considering that in Western politics , top positions in companies , and in other positions of power and influence men still out number women . I predict in future generations women will place increasingly less importance on men 's wealth , although of course we can not know this yet . Evolutionary Psychology could explain why men generally prefer younger women -LRB- Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- since youth increases the chances their mate is fertile . According to this theory , what men find attractive would be features implying youth and fertility . This is suggested by stereotypes such as obsessions with big breasts and also scientific studies ; it has been found that hip-waist ratio affects how attractive men find women -LRB- Singh , 2002 -RRB- . These theories do , as usual , make evolutionary sense . However , they are difficult to prove and there are other convincing theories such as social learning -LRB- Hyde , in Fredrickson et al , 2003 -RRB- . The example is if a boy dates an ugly girl , he will be ridiculed by friends and he and onlookers will learn to prioritise attractiveness in future . Although this might be true , this social learning theory does not explain why boys should ridicule friends more than girls , so it does not completely counter the evolutionary explanation . One controversial Evolutionary Psychology paper is Thornhill & Palmer 's -LRB- 2000 -RRB- account of rape . They claim men rape as a result of a psychological mechanism which has evolved to enable them to obtain a large number of mates in a society where females have choice . They imply that all human intercourse might once have occurred this way , by comparing our species with beetles which , they claim , also ` rape ' each other . However , I believe not only do their arguments not apply to humans , they do not make evolutionary sense . First , it has been discussed above that a high number of offspring does not guarantee survival of genes if the offspring do not reach maturity . Rape would therefore not make sense on these grounds . Second , their account does not take into account the violent nature of rape - this sexual attack is often accompanied with horrific injury to the woman - if the rapist 's sole purpose was to father a child , why would he be inclined to harm its mother ? Thirdly , the authors offer ` no data to suggest that the genes of men who rape have been selected over men who do not ' -LRB- Segal , 2001 , p. 423 -RRB- . Furthermore , Rose & Rose -LRB- 2001 -RRB- criticise the comparison of human rape with forced-sex in animals . Animals only mate with fertile members of the opposite sex . The method of cross-species comparison can not apply : human rape victims are often too young or old to be fertile , are the same sex as the attacker , or are raped anally or orally -LRB- Rose & Rose 2001 -RRB- . Conception could occur in none of these cases so would be no use ensuring the survival of the attacker 's genes . I agree with the Roses -LRB- 2001 -RRB- that Thornhill & Palmer 's -LRB- 2000 -RRB- accounts are not only misguided but also socially unethical theories to be suggesting in a society where rape victims are often terrified in trials , or too frightened to report the crime . However , Evolutionary Psychology as a discipline should not be disregarded on the grounds of this one publication , especially since I have found no other psychologists backing up Thornhill & Palmer 's claims . My final major criticism of Evolutionary Psychology is that it seems to imply that spreading our genes is the most important influence over our choices and behaviour today . However , nowadays Westerners live in a comfortable and safe society . Although we obviously avoid situations which are unsafe , everyday life is no longer a struggle for survival . Given this , we are free to enjoy our lives , with careers , hobbies , education and fun . If spreading our genes was our utmost concern why would women in the 1990s UK put off children until their 30s -LRB- Segal , 2001 -RRB- ? Although Evolutionary Psychology might help to explain motivations for behaviour we can not understand the logic of , such as irrational phobias , humans live in a different world today then in our formative years and we have adapted accordingly . In conclusion , the title quotation implies the Evolutionary Psychology approach should be our primary consideration behind psychological theorising . In my opinion it can be very useful , for example in generating hypotheses about ` why ' rather than ` how ' explanations -LRB- Campbell , 2001 , p. 426 -RRB- and should by no means be ignored . However , I would not agree with Archer -LRB- 2001 -RRB- that Evolutionary Psychology should be the ` single unifying starting point ' to explain all behaviour , because it is also equally important to take into account social and environmental factors motivating our thoughts and actions today .
This essay will discuss various advantages of using qualitative methods . I will explain why I believe that , despite controversy , qualitative methods can be valid and reliable whilst highlighting the care psychologists need to take to use them affectively . I will conclude by explaining how qualitative methods can best be used in psychological research . Burt and Oaksford * -LRB- 1999 -RRB- say scientific research involves experimentation to test a hypothesis . However , whilst in long-established physical sciences such as chemistry , ` the depth of knowledge from past theorising constrains the choice of sensible hypotheses , ' psychology remains a relatively ` new ' science -LRB- Burt & Oaksford * , 1999 -RRB- . They argue that qualitative methods are essential when generating hypothesis for research in new fields , which we are interested in but unsure which questions to ask . Without them , we could only resort to an ` armchair technique ' ; reading limited existing literature , then ` sitting around ' waiting for a hypothesis to strike you -LRB- Burt & Oaksford * , 1999 -RRB- . As a practical example , Abaunza et al -LRB- 2005 -RRB- used a grounded approach in a longitudinal study on ` the social mechanisms that encourage and discourage the uptake of smoking ' -LRB- 2005 , p. 397 -RRB- in young people . They say discourse analysis of interviews with youth-club attendees , allowed research ` from the perspective of young people themselves ' -LRB- 2005 , p. 400 -RRB- . Such research could uncover ideas the researchers would not have discovered in text books . Despite the usefulness of qualitative methods in hypothesis generation they have encountered criticism involving ` reliability ' and ` validity ' and questioning whether qualitative methods could ever pass tests for them . Reliability , put simply , is the extent to which an experiment is stable ` within itself ' -LRB- Flanagan , 2000 , p. 170 -RRB- and the likelihood of the results being repeated . Validity involves the ` truth ' of the results and whether findings could be generalised to the greater population -LRB- Flanagan , 2000 -RRB- . However , Merrick * -LRB- 1993 -RRB- points out these concepts have ` traditionally been used to access the quality of quantitative research ' . In my opinion the issue is whether these concepts should be of consideration with regard to qualitative research and if so , how should we define them ? Morgan * -LRB- 1998 -RRB- , a biologist , proposes ' a minimum attribute of good science is that it should be based upon observations that can be repeated by other competent scientists . ' However , in Coyle et al 's -LRB- 2004 -RRB- study it is hard to see how or why this could be expected . The participants were informed the study was about ` what men had to say about emotions ' -LRB- 2004 , p. 403 -RRB- and the ` interview guide was brief , relatively unstructured and non-specific ' -LRB- 2004 , p. 403 -RRB- . Clearly , a natural conversation could not be reproduced . Does this mean the results are not ` reliable ' ? Merrick -LRB- 1993 -RRB- takes a post positive position , rejecting defining ` reliability ' regarding qualitative research on the basis of replicability . Instead , she suggests we should consider how ` dependable on ' -LRB- * 1993 -RRB- the research is , by considering methods , the researchers ' backgrounds and how these would influence the results . I agree that qualitative methods can be declared ` reliable ' , and therefore useful in psychology , without using the criteria developed for quantitative studies . In my view , the concern of qualitative research is the patterns emerging from the data , which can be looked for in future similar studies , not an exact replication of what they find . Some critics also question whether qualitative research could ever be valid . Morgan maintains it is essential ` observers should try to keep personal prejudices out of science ' -LRB- * 1998 -RRB- . However in both Abaunza et al 's -LRB- 2005 -RRB- study and Coyle et al 's -LRB- 2004 -RRB- study researchers lead discussions and undoubtedly influence responses . This is ` reflexivity ... the ways in which the researcher influences the research ' -LRB- Johnson * , 1999 -RRB- . If we apply here the traditional ways of ensuring the internal validity of quantitative research - minimising extraneous variables so it is certain the Independent Variable is what is influencing the Dependent Variable - then Morgan would be right . However , in qualitative studies validity should not be so simply defined . To illustrate , Hammersly and Atkinson -LRB- 1983 , as cited by Johnson , 1999 -RRB- regard reflexivity as an important aspect of research because ` researchers should set about understanding the effects they will have * . ' In Coyle et al 's -LRB- 2004 -RRB- study they discuss ways they personally could have influenced interpretation ; ` we drew on our familiarity with mainstream and critical European social psychology ... our different gender positions also meant that we positioned ourselves differently in relation to the text and the speakers ' -LRB- p. 404 -RRB- . I believe as long as researchers give as much relevant information as to how they may have influenced research , and do not try to pass off results as objective truth ` out there ' -LRB- a concept which Morgan , 1998 , p. 488 thinks science should involve -RRB- , the results can still be internally valid . Furthermore , I agree with Woolgar -LRB- 1996 , cited by Johnson , 1999 -RRB- that reflexivity is a problem in all research , whether quantitative or qualitative - all interpretation of results will depend on who is interpreting them and why . Merick -LRB- 1993 * -RRB- says that in qualitative research , ` reliability and validity depend on the relationship between the researcher and research purpose ... -LRB- and -RRB- between the researcher and the interpretive community . ' Therefore psychologists must be aware of these relationships and take these into account , so that qualitative research does not mislead . The last major consideration is external validity - if qualitative results are dependent on context , how could we generalise to the greater population ? Johnson -LRB- 1999 -RRB- points out that making generalisations is not usually the intent of qualitative research . For example , discourse analysis ` recognises that objectivity is never actually obtainable and that intense scrutiny made of each discourse produces data that is as biased -LRB- or unbiased -RRB- as any other form of research ' -LRB- Flanagan , 2000 , p. 130 -RRB- . Normally more research would be necessary before any generalisations could be made and psychologists should keep this in mind . Another advantage of using Qualitative methods is when studying groups . For example when studying ` men 's talk about emotions ' -LRB- Coyle et al , 2004 , p. 401 -RRB- the ` placing of an individual , in relation to one or two others ' -LRB- p. 402 -RRB- was of interest . In my opinion it would be ridiculous to suggest that a quantitative method such as a questionnaire would be a more ` valid ' or ` reliable ' way to study individuals in groups than discourse analysis . Finally , qualitative research may be of use to psychologists when quantitative methods are not practical or are simply unethical . An example is Burt 's postgraduate research on treating survivors of child sexual abuse -LRB- unpublished , cited by Burt & Oaksford , 1999 -RRB- . They point out that to study this quantitatively , they would need unbiased samples of people who were or were not abused and who are now adjusted or maladjusted . Ethically , survivors can only take part voluntarily , and it unlikely many would come forward for laboratory testing . However , most respond well to discussions in a ` nurturing environment ' -LRB- Burt & Oaksford * , 1999 -RRB- and good treatment methods have been derived from this source . As Burt and Oaksford -LRB- 1999 -RRB- point out , there is a pressing need for such studies ; society needs answers to psychological questions such as ` how do we best treat survivors of abuse ? ' and ` how can we stop young people starting smoking ? ' right now , and right now qualitative methods appear to be productive methods to reaching answers for them . In conclusion , qualitative methods can best be used by psychologists to generate hypotheses in new areas , study groups , develop treatments , and as an alternative when quantitative tests are not possible . However , if conducting the research psychologists must give detailed information so that the study can be considered in the appropriate context . If reading a qualitative study , psychologists must be careful not to over-generalise . Under these guidelines , qualitative research is very valuable in psychology .
This essay will first explain what stage theories are and then give an outline of the theory of Piaget , the major stage theorist with regard to developmental psychology . Various criticisms of Piaget will then be discussed . Vygotsky 's stage theory will be mentioned , with regard to its similarities and differences to Piaget 's , as will the implications these comparisons have on how stage theories should be considered in general . We will then look at the influence Piaget has had on other psychological stage models . Finally , I will conclude that although stage models do not explain all there is to know about development , they are important ways of studying the changes in cognitive ability as a human matures . Harris & Butterworth -LRB- p. 41 : 2002 -RRB- define stages in development as meaning each stage involves some sort of change caused by development . They cite Flavell -LRB- 19623 -RRB- whose criteria for a stage theory were : Qualitative changes at each stage , so the child either does something new or does something in a novel way . His example is crawling becoming walking - a stage of motor development . The new ability acquired must be manifested across a wide range of behaviours . The example is when children acquire the concept of symbolic representation they show this not only by learning to speak -LRB- words are symbols -RRB- but also through play -LRB- a doll becomes a person -RRB- . The transition between stages should be relatively quick , for example the changes at adolescence often occur together . Piaget is a stage theorist , whose theory emerged through studying his own children and keeping diaries , providing him with detailed longitudinal information . It could be argued that this method could provoke bias or misrepresentation . However , Piaget also conducted experiments on other children based on his findings , providing cross-sectional data too . In order to examine the advantages and disadvantages of his theory , I will go through each of the four main stages and asses Piaget 's claims about each one . The first main stage is sensori-motor , from birth until around 2 years , where baies discover ` the relationships between their actions and the consequences ' -LRB- Fredrickson et al , p. 76 : 2003 -RRB- . By touching and observing they learn to distinguish themselves from their environment . Piaget observes the ` reflex reaction ' ; the baby will automatically such , thus make new discoveries about even non-food objects , such as her hand , and she will elarn from this experiment . Piaget strongly belived that babies were incluned to test out their world and assimilate new knowledge they gained thought this -LRB- Fredrickson et al , p. 75 : 2003 -RRB- . Piaget believes this is when babies acquire the concept of object permanence , i.e. the knowledge that hings not immediately present can still exist . This clian is based on tests which involved placing a toy in front of an eight-month-old baby , who reacts to it , but shows no surprise or attempt to search for it when it is then hidden behing a screen . This is taken as evidence that the baby is acting ` as if the toy no longer exists ' -LRB- Fredrickson et al , p. 74 : 2003 -RRB- . It is not until a baby reaches 10 months that they are seen to search . However , Piaget 's conclusion about the baby 's thoughts seems tome to be based entirely on her action . Perhaps the baby dos realise the toy been hidden , but she is simply not yet aware of her own ability to search or has no desire to do so . It seems presumptuous to assume she has no concept of object permanence . Furthermore , Baillargeon & Graber -LRB- 1987 -RRB- said Piaget 's tasks were too demanding . Their ` violation of expectations ' test did not rely on motor ability -LRB- i.e. obvious searching -RRB- but instead studied preferential looking . Infants were shown sequences involving rabbits . In one there was an ` impossible event ' where there was a gap in the wall the rabbit was behind which should have allowed viewing of the ears but they did n't appear . Babies of just five-and-a-half months looked longer at this ` impossible event ' , suggesting they understood the rabbit still existed behind the wall . However , although this study could show faults in Piaget 's method it does little damage to stage theory ; object permanence is still suggested to be a concept which is n't possessed by the newborn but is acquired is the early stages of development . After the sensori-motor stage is the pre-operational stage , from around ages 2 1/2 - 6 , where ` children gradually acquire systematic , logical thinking ' -LRB- Butterworth & Harris , p. 185 : 2002 -RRB- . This is where they take the abilities they have concerning the physical world , like separating and combining , and apply these to their own thoughts . ` Although the young child can reason about simple problems , the system of mental operations lacks critical linkages ' -LRB- Butterworth & Harris p. 185 : 2002 -RRB- . This means that the child recognises two rows of 10 coins are the ` same ' if they 're spaced equally , but if one is stretched then it will contain ` more ' . Piaget saw this as evidence the child lacked the concept of conservation . . However , critics such as Donaldson -LRB- 1978 -RRB- and Siegal -LRB- 1997 -RRB- do not agree that children are incapable of logical thinking , but believe their mistakes at this stage are due to Piaget 's confusing tasks , which do not make ` human sense . ' In a Donaldson 's task , where ` naughty teddy moved the counters ' 71.9 % of children in the pre-operational stage showed conservations , as apposed to Piaget 's 33.6 % . The next stage is the concrete operational stage -LRB- 7-11 years -RRB- where Piaget believes the child ` achieves conservation of number , age , and weight ' -LRB- Fredrickson et al , p. 75 : 2003 -RRB- . Here abstract terms can be understood , but only with regard to concrete objects . It is only in the formal operational stage , reached around adolescence , where we are able to think in ` purely symbolic terms ' -LRB- Fredrickson et al , p. 75 : 2003 -RRB- . Whilst the points detailed above may be fair criticisms of Piaget 's method , they do not suggest the fundamental idea of stages to be wrong , so we will now take a look at Vygotsky , another stage theorist . His stages coincided with Piaget 's . although the approach was slightly different . Whilst Piaget was a trained biologist , Vygotsky concentrated on social and environmental influences . Concerning the relationship between language and thought , they also differ . Vygotsky placed more importance on the roles of social interaction on the development of thought -LRB- Butterworth & Harris p. 27 : 2002 -RRB- and concentrated more on the typical ages behaviour emerged than making assumptions bout how developed the mind was at this stage . Despite having different research interests , Vygotsky and Piaget make similar stage divisions . Respectively , birth-2 years is affiliation/sensori-motor stage , 2-7 years is play/preoperational stage , 7-12 years is learning/concrete operational stage and adolescence through to adulthood is work/formal operational stage . These common transition periods suggest that there are a number of obvious qualitative changes occurring at each boundary , so do support the idea that some sort of stage model is appropriate even if there were flaws in those of either psychologist . Early stage theorists such as Piaget have influenced the work of many psychologists . Smilansky 's play stages -LRB- 1968 -RRB- largely fit in with Piaget 's cognitive development stages . Her ` functional play ' stage , where children do things with their toys -LRB- like dialling a phone -RRB- is in accordance with Piaget 's Preoperational stage ; the ` constructive play ' stage -LRB- like building a tower -RRB- , followed by ` socio-dramatic play ' stage -LRB- make believe games -RRB- and then finally ` games with rules ' stage all show a progression in understanding of abstract circumstances with age . The transition ages are similar to those of Piaget 's stages , reiterating what he believed to be the cognitive capabilities of children of specific ages . Kohlberg -LRB- 1966 & 1969 -RRB- , who produced a stage model of the development of gender identity , was also heavily influenced by Piaget . His stages show a progression from a two year old being aware of sex , but believing it can change -LRB- e.g. a boy in a skirt ` becomes ' a girl -RRB- to realising it remains the same over time and across different situations . This follows the theoretical foundations laid by Piaget , who believed that abstract concepts such as time were acquired in later stages of development . The fact that very similar patterns emerge from these four stage theories discussed , which were developed at different times by psychologists with different research interests , is a strong argument for why stage theories should be supported . However , despite their merits there still remain some problems with stage theories . First , the impression is given that most children go through the transitions at the same ages . In fact , development occurs at quite different rates for all children . Piaget also failed to acknowledge that whilst some forms of development are maturationally controlled , such as puberty , others can -LRB- to some extent -RRB- be hastened . For example , the Kipsigis tribe in Kenya ` teach ' their young to sit up , stand and walk by tapping into the ` stepping reflex ' babies have when their feet touch a surface -LRB- Cole & Cole 2001 , as cited by Fredrickson et al , p. 68 : 2003 -RRB- and these babies reach such milestones around 4 weeks before Americans do . However , the stage theorists are still correct in that all children do go through such stages , and that the stages must occur in the specific order . Another critical aspect that the stage theorists appear to ignore is the importance of social interaction for normal development . For example , Chomsky 's theory of language acquisition -LRB- 1968 -RRB- involves a language acquisition device -LRB- L.A.D -RRB- which he believes we possess innately , but requires exposure to adult speech to be stimulated . Likewise , Lennenberg 's critical period theory -LRB- 1976 -RRB- highlights the importance of language-learning at a specific stage -LRB- before puberty -RRB- because at puberty ` lateralisation ' occurs and language will not progress past basic stages . In conclusion , the advantages of stage models of cognitive development are that they appear to log essential pathway s all humans must go through throughout maturation , and highlight that the order certain milestones occur is universal . They also illustrate our developing capacities of reasoning and how the way we think changes as we mature . Despite several problems with Piaget 's experimental methods , and perhaps the over-simplification of the stage boundaries , stage models have proved to be successful and donsistent ways to study cognitive development .
